# Why FP8 Rollouts Can Change Learning: Jet-RL, Long Tails, and Asynchrony

Why can a rollout engine and trainer assign different probabilities to the same token with identical weights? The main reading, Jet-RL, explains quantization scales, aligned forward precision flows, and residual error. Optional readings cover TLT’s drafter training during long-tail idle periods and VCPO’s control of variance from stale-policy samples. Start with the probability example and Figure 5; explore the companions as needed. The methods address different problems, and their speedup ratios cannot simply be multiplied.

## Main reading · Jet-RL: Enabling On-Policy FP8 Reinforcement Learning with Unified Training and Rollout Precision Flow

**Year / publication：** 2026 · arXiv 2026 · NVIDIA / MIT / UC Berkeley / independent researcher · v1 2026-01-20; v2 2026-01-24 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14243v2>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth close reading: Figure 5 and Section 4.1, then Section 4.2 and Tables 2–4; compare the failures in Section 3 and identify remaining consistency requirements.

**Why revisit：** Previously mentioned in a research-direction overview; this note develops the precision flow, backward computation, experimental conditions, and limits of numerical agreement.

### Identical weights can still disagree about a generated token

Consider an LLM solving a math problem. Its rollout engine uses FP8, an 8-bit floating-point format, for selected computation to generate answers faster. The trainer uses BF16, a 16-bit format, to recompute those answers’ probabilities before updating the model from their rewards. Even immediately synchronized weights need not produce identical probabilities when rounding and quantization occur at different places.

Here is an illustrative example, not a measurement: a sampled token has probability 0.20 in the rollout engine and 0.16 when the trainer recomputes it. Dividing the training probability by the actual behavior probability gives 0.8 before any weight update. That discrepancy is numerical, not entirely a consequence of learning. Jet-RL aims to reduce this source of disagreement while preserving the speed benefit of FP8.

### Separate quantization, stale weights, and random sampling

A policy is the distribution over the next token conditioned on the existing text. On-policy learning uses data generated by the current policy being targeted. Practical RL also reuses data and applies importance corrections, so a policy difference is not automatically an implementation error. The important questions are which distribution produced the data and how the objective handles it.

Weights that lag several updates cause temporal policy differences. Different numerical implementations of the same weight version cause execution differences. Even equal distributions can generate different tokens under random sampling. Our diagnostic recommendation is to fix weights and token prefixes, then compare probabilities at the same positions before sampling. Comparing only two freely generated answers confounds these causes.

### FP8 is more than a storage dtype

FP8 represents a limited set of values. Quantization typically chooses a scale, divides values by it, and rounds to representable numbers; computation then accounts for the scale. Jet-RL uses E4M3, whose maximum finite value is 448. With a coarse scale, a few large values can consume the available range and leave small values poorly represented.

The paper therefore uses local groups: 1×128 groups for forward activations and 128×128 blocks for weights. Think of one activation row as a token’s features: each consecutive group of 128 features shares a scale, rather than every token and feature sharing one. Grouping is part of executed model behavior, so matching the label FP8 alone does not establish equivalent computation.

### The central change: train through the same low-precision forward path

A BF16-training / FP8-rollout setup quantizes linear-layer inputs and weights in the generator while retaining a BF16 training forward path. Jet-RL instead aligns the forward precision flow: transferred dtypes, quantization locations, and group sizes. Figure 5 depicts the inference computation inside the training graph, with matching precision and granularity on its edges.

Training still keeps BF16 master weights for parameter updates and derives quantized weights for forward computation. This avoids forcing every small update directly into FP8’s coarse representable values. Inference weights are quantized during parameter updates, while activation quantization can be fused into the preceding operator. This describes the paper’s Section 4 design, not a claim that arbitrary current verl or vLLM configurations implement it automatically.

### Accelerate backward GEMMs without making the whole path FP8

A linear layer needs both a weight gradient and an input gradient in addition to its forward matrix multiplication. The paper accelerates those backward multiplications with FP8 operands and BF16 outputs. Gradients transferred between operators remain BF16 to reduce underflow and quantization noise. Some forward activations saved for backward use remain FP8.

Backward products use matrices in different orientations. An activation quantized by row may need column-oriented grouping in another product, requiring requantization rather than just an indexing transpose. Symmetric 128×128 weight blocks are easier to reuse after transposition. One source caveat matters: the v2 Figure 6 caption and Section 4.2 text reverse the assignment of the two layouts to DGrad and WGrad. We do not turn that inconsistency into a copyable kernel recipe; an implementation must check Section 2.2 dimensions against actual code.

### What mismatch does this address, and what remains unproven?

Jet-RL primarily aligns forward differences introduced by linear-layer quantization. Matching that design does not automatically fix reduction order, attention implementations, batch shapes, KV-cache precision, or distributed numerical behavior. Expert routing needs its own checks in MoE models; the main training evaluations here do not establish large-scale MoE routing agreement.

The supported conclusion is that the unified precision flow improves stability and narrows quality gaps to BF16 in the tested configurations. It is not a proof of bitwise equality across all layers, batches, and inference engines. When comparing this work with a zero-mismatch reference, first identify which source of error is aligned before asking whether stronger exact-equality requirements hold.

### Evaluation: models, tasks, and the quality gap that remains

The paper evaluates models including Llama3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Qwen3-8B-Base on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Training uses GSM8K plus MATH, or harder DeepMATH tasks, with four responses per prompt for the mixture and sixteen for DeepMATH. It reports a learning rate of 10⁻⁶, batch size 256, and KL coefficient 10⁻³. The KL term limits deviation from a reference policy. Baselines are BF16 and BF16 training with FP8 rollouts.

Table 2 gives a useful concrete comparison: for Qwen3-8B-Base trained on GSM8K+MATH with 8K rollouts, the four listed benchmark averages are 63.8 for BF16, 60.9 for the mixed setup, and 62.7 for Jet-RL. The remaining gap is 1.1 percentage points. In Table 3’s 16K setting for the same model, the mixed baseline does not converge; Jet-RL scores 61.3 versus BF16’s 64.0, a 2.7-point gap. Stable training and zero quality loss are separate requirements.

These are author-reported task results, not a theorem that every FP8-rollout setup must fail. Some model names and captions in Section 3 also conflict with its prose, so this note uses the explicit configurations in Tables 2–3 for numerical comparisons. Replication should add seed variability and distinguish a response-length cap from actual generated length.

### Separate rollout throughput, step time, and total learning cost

Table 4 benchmarks vLLM on H100s using 512 prompts, 512 input tokens each, and maximum concurrency 128. For the 32B configuration with TP=2 and 8K output length, FP8 delivers 1.33× BF16 token throughput; at TP=4 and the same length it delivers 1.07×. Tensor parallelism distributes model computation across GPUs. Communication and other unaccelerated work can dilute faster low-precision multiplication.

A separate end-to-end result for Qwen3-8B with 8K rollouts reports a 1.16× RL step-time speedup. It does not establish 33% faster training universally, and the 32B standalone inference gain is not a full 32B RL gain; the paper leaves larger-model training scaling to future work. Our systems recommendation is also to measure hours to a matched evaluation target, since both step time and the number of required steps determine total learning cost.

### A concrete question to investigate after reading

Here is a proposed experiment, not a result from the paper. Freeze weights and token prefixes, then compare BF16/BF16, BF16/FP8, and aligned FP8 training/rollout pairs. Record per-position log-probability differences, distribution distances, and outlier rates while changing batch size, sequence length, parallelism, and attention implementation one at a time. This asks where residual disagreement comes from after FP8 alignment.

Introduce policy-version lag as a separate factor afterward. Changing precision, asynchrony, and sampling settings together makes failures hard to attribute. Only then run short RL experiments to test whether numerical diagnostics predict learning outcomes. This connects systems debugging to a research question: how much agreement is actually needed for stable learning?

### Reading route and primary source

Start with Figure 5 and Section 4.1 to distinguish BF16 master weights from FP8 forward computation. Then read Section 4.2 for scales and backward layouts, followed by Tables 2–4 for stability, quality gaps, and throughput. Derive Section 2.2’s three matrix products on a second pass; a full GRPO derivation is not a prerequisite for the first reading.

## Optional reading · Taming the Long-Tail: Efficient Reasoning RL Training with Adaptive Drafter

**Year / publication：** 2026 · ASPLOS 2026 · MIT / NVIDIA / ETH Zurich / MIT-IBM AI Lab / UMass Amherst · first preprint 2025-11-20; v3 2026-03-20 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16665v3>)

**Reading guidance：** Read Figure 8 and Section 4.2 closely to establish the drafter’s data sources, training resources, and preemption boundary. Then read Figure 14, Section 5, and Section 6.1 for scheduling behavior and measurement conditions.

**Why revisit：** Moving from a research overview to mechanism-level reading: compare training a drafter during long-tail idle periods with Jet-RL’s low-precision acceleration.

### Why can an entire training step wait after most answers are finished?

Consider our teaching example: four GPU groups generate candidate solutions to a batch of math problems. Three groups finish, while the fourth still has two problems whose solutions keep exploring alternatives. The main model cannot update until all responses finish. The first three groups wait; the fourth remains busy but processes very few simultaneous requests. This generation stage is called rollout. A few long responses determine when the whole step ends, creating two forms of waste: idle GPU groups and poor utilization on the groups still decoding. [Figure 1 and Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S2)

TLT addresses both: advance the remaining requests by multiple tokens at a time, and use finished workers to train a small auxiliary model that predicts upcoming tokens. This model is the drafter. The main model still waits and updates according to the original RL workflow. It is the auxiliary drafter that trains early, not the main policy using an incomplete response batch. This distinguishes the design from asynchronous RL that permits stale-policy samples in policy updates. [Sections 3 and 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S3)

### Let a small model propose, then let the large model verify

Ordinary generation advances one token at a time. Speculative decoding first lets the drafter propose several candidates cheaply, then verifies them in parallel with one forward pass of the main model. Accepted candidates advance the output; unsuitable candidates are not retained merely because the drafter proposed them. Drafting accuracy therefore primarily affects speed, while the main model’s verification rules govern the output. The paper emphasizes preserving the target sampling distribution. This is an algorithmic objective, not evidence that every GPU kernel and precision configuration produces bitwise-identical results. [Section 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S2.SS2)

RL introduces a complication absent from serving a fixed model: the main model changes after each policy update, so a previously useful drafter can become less accurate. TLT defaults to an EAGLE-style single-layer drafter. Its trainable core is one Transformer decoder block containing attention and a feed-forward network. It reuses the main model’s embedding and output layers, freezing those reused parts during drafter training. It reads internal vectors from the main model to predict later tokens and continues learning as RL progresses. The point is not merely to select a complete language model with fewer parameters, but to reduce the number of layers the drafter must execute sequentially so that proposals are cheap. [Section 4.1 and Figure 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S4.SS1)

### Train during idle periods without learning only from short answers

Return to the four GPU groups. A coordinator switches a group to auxiliary training only after it finishes rollout and releases inference memory. One worker may comprise a whole GPU group running one model instance, rather than a single GPU. Auxiliary training uses completed responses and internal vectors produced by the main model. Once the remaining long responses finish, the coordinator stops the auxiliary work so the primary RL workflow can continue. The paper calls this preemptible use of idle resources the Spot Trainer. It also saves only the drafter’s trainable parts and writes checkpoints asynchronously to reduce work lost at preemption. [Section 4.2 and Figure 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S4.SS2)

Training only on the earliest completions would rarely expose the drafter to the long responses it most needs to accelerate. TLT therefore retains a DataBuffer across RL steps, combining current completed responses with long responses from the preceding step. Cached internal vectors reduce repeated large-model computation. Previous-step data are slightly stale, but train the auxiliary model rather than being inserted directly into the current main-policy RL update. Using idle resources also does not make computation free: the paper reports additional memory use, reprocessing prefixes when speculative decoding is activated, and occasional resource contention. [Sections 4.2 and 6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S6.SS4)

### Propose more when few requests remain, and sometimes less when many remain

At the start of a step, the main model already processes many requests together. Expanding many candidates per request can add computation, exhaust memory, or slow execution. When only a few requests remain, extra candidates can make better use of otherwise idle compute. TLT enables speculative decoding below a default threshold of 32 remaining requests and continues adjusting draft depth and the number of tokens sent for verification. Its selector groups configurations by batch size, occasionally tries alternatives, and otherwise favors a recently measured high-throughput choice. This exploration–exploitation procedure is a multi-armed bandit. Its objective is useful token progress per unit time, rather than acceptance rate alone. [Sections 5.1–5.2 and Figure 14](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S5)

Switching configurations also creates CUDA Graph memory costs. CUDA Graphs record sequences of GPU operations to reduce repeated launch overhead, but different shapes often require separate recordings. TLT groups nearby batch sizes, captures the drafter and main model separately, and merges reusable captures instead of allocating a full additional recording for every combination. Before a trained drafter is available, it can retrieve common short token sequences from responses to the same prompt and offer them as candidates for main-model verification. This is a retrieval-based fallback, not unconditional answer copying. [Section 5 and Figure 10](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S5)

### What became faster, and which costs does that number not answer?

Experiments primarily use up to eight DGX H100 servers, totaling 64 GPUs, with additional A100 evaluations. Models include Qwen2.5-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. The main training data are a math-and-coding subset of Eurus-2-RL. Training uses GRPO, which forms a learning signal by comparing responses to the same question, with sampling temperature 0.9, a 32K-token response limit, and BF16 training. A subset of OpenThoughts2-1M separately warms up the drafter. Baselines include the paper’s versions of VeRL and Open-R1, plus TLT-Base with the adaptive drafter disabled. [Section 6.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S6.SS1)

Figure 11 reports roughly 1.7–2.1× end-to-end throughput relative to VeRL. The metric divides all prompt and response tokens in a global batch by one RL step’s duration, averages only three steps after warm-up, and uses a trained drafter. It includes generation and training within a step, but does not establish total savings across initialization, warm-up, and full convergence. Scale also matters: Table 3 reports Qwen2.5-7B increasing from 1.21× on one node to 1.76× on eight nodes. The two Qwen models’ similar average reward curves in Figure 12 support no obvious degradation of the measured training behavior; they do not guarantee identical final capability on every task. [Sections 6.1–6.3, Figures 11–12, and Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16665v3#S6)

### Turn the mechanism into a concrete systems question

Start with Figure 8 and draw separate timelines for main-model and drafter training. Next read Figure 14 to see why speculative strategies change as requests finish. Finish with Section 6.1’s measurement procedure and Section 7’s scope. Section 7 observes that an agent waiting for a tool may retain its KV cache without performing GPU decoding, reducing active request counts and potentially favoring speculation. This is a proposed extension, not a multi-turn tool experiment completed in the paper. Useful follow-up questions include how much cache to retain during the wait, whether a whole worker is actually available for training, and how much memory remains for candidate verification. These are research questions suggested by the design, not settings to which its speedup numbers can simply be transferred.

## Optional reading · Stable Asynchrony: Variance-Controlled Off-Policy RL for LLMs

**Year / publication：** 2026 · ICML 2026 · MIT / NVIDIA · PMLR 306 · [Original source](<https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/huang26ca.html>)

**Reading guidance：** Read Sections 2.2 and 3.2–3.5 closely. Start with Figures 1–2, then check Table 1 and Appendices B and F.4.

**Why revisit：** Moving from a research overview to mechanism-level reading: distinguish numerical implementation differences from update variance caused by stale-policy samples alongside Jet-RL.

### Why can a good answer from an older model destabilize training?

Imagine one machine still generating answers with model version 10 while another has updated the parameters to version 12. Parameters are the numerical values governing predictions; the two versions can assign different token probabilities to the same question. These are stale-policy samples: the policy generating the answers differs from the policy being trained. Asynchrony lets both machines work concurrently, reducing waiting but sustaining this mismatch.

Training commonly multiplies a sample by an importance weight w: the current policy’s probability of its complete answer divided by the actual sampling policy’s probability. This corrects for answers coming from a different distribution. As a teaching example, an answer twenty times more probable under the current policy can receive weight 20. Sequence probabilities multiply token probabilities, so small shifts can accumulate over long responses. An update may consequently depend mostly on a few trajectories despite having many samples; replacing the batch can substantially change its direction and magnitude. This is high variance, not proof that the dominant answers are incorrect. [Sections 2.1–2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S2.SS2)

### First control: take a smaller step when few samples dominate

VCPO measures weight concentration using effective sample size, ESS=(Σw)²/Σ(w²). Equal weights across B samples give ESS=B; near-total domination by one sample gives ESS close to one. ESS diagnoses weight degeneracy: it does not recount correct answers or provide an unconditional exact measurement of gradient variance. The authors observe falling ESS ratios before policy divergence and performance collapse, motivating more cautious updates.

Let ρ=ESS/B. The learning rate becomes η_eff=η×√(ρ_off/ρ_on), where the learning rate controls the size of the parameter move and ρ_on is estimated from one or a few initial synchronous updates rather than universally set to one. For example, if the reference is one and a batch of 100 has ESS=4, the rate becomes 0.2 times the original. This is an empirically supported scaling rule, not a theorem guaranteeing stability. The final update also truncates large weights, but ESS uses the untruncated weights so truncation does not hide their concentration. [Sections 3.2 and 3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S3.SS2)

### Second control: which reference should be subtracted from reward?

A reward baseline b is a reference subtracted from R: learning uses R−b instead of the absolute reward alone. A common choice is the group mean reward. VCPO accounts for unequal influence under asynchrony by weighting rewards with w²×||g||² before averaging. Here g is the parameter-gradient direction for increasing a trajectory’s likelihood, and ||g|| measures its magnitude. Trajectories with both large importance weights and large gradients have more influence on the variance-reduction baseline. [Section 3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S3.SS3)

The “minimum variance” claim has a defined scope. Appendix B derives the optimum over scalar baselines under assumptions including exact importance sampling. The practical minibatch formula substitutes sample averages for population expectations. Full VCPO also caps sequence importance weights at eight, so the complete algorithm should not be described as strictly unbiased. Nor is the baseline an additional model predicting each step’s value: no separate critic is trained. The final version acknowledges earlier derivations of this optimal-baseline form; its practical contribution includes efficient exact per-trajectory gradient statistics combined with ESS-guided step scaling. [Final version, Sections 3.3–3.5 and Appendix B](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v306/main/assets/huang26ca/huang26ca.pdf#page=6)

### Obtaining the statistics without a second backward pass

A naive implementation first computes per-trajectory gradient norms, obtains the baseline, and then backpropagates again. The paper instead decomposes the update into two accumulators: one sums wR·g and the other sums w·g. Each trajectory is backpropagated once while recording these quantities and its gradient norm. Once b is known, the final gradient combines “first accumulator minus b times the second.” Data-parallel gradient synchronization is deferred until local trajectory statistics have been computed and the final gradient assembled. [Section 3.4 and Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S3.SS4)

This still costs resources. Figure 3 reports 19% step-time overhead and 14% memory overhead for Qwen2.5-7B on four H100 GPUs with tensor parallelism TP=4 and sequence length 8,192. The method removes the naive extra backward pass, not all overhead. Its value therefore depends on total training time to a target quality, rather than the speed of one update alone.

### What do the 2.5× speedup and 128-step lag actually establish?

The most agent-like experiment trains Qwen2.5-7B Base on DAPO-17K with SimpleTIR-style interleaved reasoning and tool calls, evaluating final-answer accuracy on AIME 2025. Each batch has 128 prompts and 16 responses per prompt, with a 12,288-token response cap and at most five tool calls. Two B200 GPUs generate rollouts and two train. Policy lag is capped at two updates. In Figure 1, VCPO takes approximately 42 hours to match the best validation accuracy reached by synchronous training at approximately 105 hours: about 2.5× faster time to that quality. This is neither a 2.5× generation-throughput claim nor a pure systems comparison at equal update counts. [Figure 1, Section 4.5, and Appendix F.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S4.SS5)

A separate experiment raises Qwen2.5-7B’s policy lag to 128 updates to stress-test learning stability, as shown in Figure 6. The authors report that asynchronous speed gains in the relevant experiments already saturate around a lag of ten; increasing lag further mainly tests robustness. The result does not establish that tool agents can safely tolerate a 128-step lag, and 128 is not a speedup factor. [Section 4.4 and Figure 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S4.SS4)

### Connecting this to today’s main reading, and where to read next

Reading VCPO alongside Jet-RL separates two issues: better numerical agreement between training and generation does not prevent asynchronous samples from coming from older parameters. VCPO studies how to control the resulting update variance. This connection is reading analysis, not a reported experiment combining the methods. Main effectiveness experiments use standard-precision dense Transformers from 1.5B to 7B; the final version adds collapse diagnostics with Llama3.1-8B Instruct on ORZ math. The authors leave MoE, FP8, and longer sparse-reward agent tasks to future work. Start with Figures 1–2, then Sections 2.2 and 3.2–3.5. For implementation, read Algorithm 1 and Appendix C; for the mathematical scope, read Appendix B. [Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17616v2#S5)
