## Main reading · HybridFlow: A Flexible and Efficient RLHF Framework

**Year / publication：** 2025 · EuroSys 2025 · preprint 2024 · [Original source](<https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf>)

**Reading guidance：** Main reading for this edition. Spend about 30 minutes mapping GPU allocation and weight movement across generation, preparation, and training, then read the controller design and 3D-HybridEngine; connect them to a familiar rollout/refit pipeline.

### Two-minute overview: the unit of optimization is an RL iteration

HybridFlow is a EuroSys 2025 paper from the University of Hong Kong and ByteDance, first released as a 2024 preprint, and the paper behind verl. Its three threads are a hybrid control model for RL dataflows, low-redundancy transitions between actor training and generation layouts, and model placement chosen for iteration latency. It merits a close read because faster rollout alone does not guarantee faster training: transfers and transitions can consume the saving. [Paper §3, Fig. 4](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=6)

### Background: why optimizing one model is insufficient

Think of PPO as several cooperating services. The actor generates responses; a reference policy supplies reference probabilities; a reward model scores responses; a critic estimates values; actor and critic are then updated. Their dependencies, memory requirements, and useful parallel configurations differ. Generation repeatedly executes token steps, whereas training also maintains gradients and optimizer states and performs backward computation. One parallel configuration may suit one stage and impede another. [Paper §2.1–2.3, Fig. 1–3](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=3)

A node here is a distributed model computation in the dataflow graph. The paper's intra-node and inter-node terminology means within or between graph nodes, not necessarily within or between physical servers. An actor node can itself span multiple machines.

The needed parallelism vocabulary is small: data parallelism (DP) assigns different samples to replicas; tensor parallelism (TP) partitions a layer’s tensor computation across devices; pipeline parallelism (PP) places layers in different stages. The discussion follows layouts and data paths without requiring a full derivation of the PPO loss first.

### Follow one batch of prompts through an iteration

**1. Generate.** Distribute prompts across the actor's generation replicas and sample responses with the current policy. Relevant concerns include concurrency, KV capacity, and the work assigned to each replica.

**2. Prepare experience.** Send prompts and responses to the reference, reward, and critic computations to obtain reference log probabilities, rewards, and values, then form advantages and other training inputs. PPO also needs probabilities associated with the sampling policy; the official programming example explicitly computes old_log_prob. Different DP sizes require redistributing data according to the consumer's layout. [Paper Fig. 1 and Fig. 6](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=7); [official PPO example](https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/hybrid_flow.html#ppo-main-loop)

**3. Update.** Train the actor and critic using that experience. Computations sharing GPUs normally run sequentially; independent resources can execute ready work concurrently. **4. Prepare the next rollout.** Make updated actor weights available in the generation engine's required layout. The reference policy does not simply track every actor update, and the four-model PPO graph should not be imposed unchanged on algorithms such as GRPO or ReMax.

### Hybrid control: separate orchestration from distributed computation

A single controller makes a sequence such as generate, score, and update easy to express, but centrally dispatching a model's large operator graph incurs overhead. Multiple controllers execute distributed computation locally with low dispatch overhead, but computation, collectives, and transfers between models can become tangled. HybridFlow uses one controller between models and multiple controllers inside each distributed model. [Paper §2.2, §4, Fig. 2](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=3)

ParallelWorker encapsulates computation; ResourcePool specifies placement; transfer protocols specify collection and distribution. In Fig. 5, the controller passes data futures, and destination workers fetch the portions required by their DP ranks. The central arrows should not be read as routing every large tensor through the controller CPU. Asynchronous invocation and dependency-driven execution also do not by themselves imply fully asynchronous RL across policy versions. [Paper §4.1, Fig. 5](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=7)

### 3D-HybridEngine: why change TP, PP, and DP between stages?

The paper time-shares the same actor GPUs between training and generation while allowing different layouts. Training uses (p,t,d), denoting PP, TP, and DP. Generation uses (p_g,t_g,d_g,d), where d_g is the number of generation micro-DP replicas created from each training replica. Device-count conservation gives N_a = ptd = p_g t_g d_g d, hence d_g = pt/(p_g t_g). [Paper §5.1–5.2, Fig. 7](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=8)

Reducing generation TP or PP can create more replicas and reduce some communication, but changes the model and KV burden per GPU. Smaller TP is therefore not universally faster. Fig. 15 demonstrates this tradeoff on 16 GPUs with training layout (1,8,2): the favorable generation TP differs between the 7B and 13B models.

### The mechanism: generation shards retain the local training shard

Fig. 8 uses eight GPUs. Training TP groups are [G1,G2,G3,G4] and [G5,G6,G7,G8], and generation TP is reduced to two. Keeping adjacent ranks as generation TP groups misaligns some GPUs' existing training shards with their required generation shards, creating extra storage. HybridFlow instead uses generation TP groups such as [G1,G3] and [G2,G4], and all-gathers within micro-DP groups such as [G1,G2] and [G3,G4]. Each GPU retains its training shard and gathers the additional pieces needed for generation. [Paper §5.3, Fig. 8](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=8)

![Simplified layout for one training DP replica: TP changes from four to two, with generation groups G1/G3 and G2/G4; each GPU retains its local training shard after all-gather.](EN/reading/figures/hybridflow-layout.png)

This original schematic illustrates the principle in the paper’s Fig. 8 using four GPUs from one training replica. A/B/C/D denote weight shards; KV, gradients, and optimizer state are excluded. Follow which local shard each GPU retains and which GPUs together form a complete generation replica.

Zero redundancy in Table 2 refers to extra GPU weight storage caused by resharding: peak parameter storage equals the generation partition M/(t_g p_g). It does not mean zero communication, zero additional memory for the entire system, or a single physical buffer. Section 7 explicitly describes separate training and generation buffers, CPU offload, and NCCL collection. Moving KV storage through CPU memory also does not establish that old activations remain valid after weights change. [Paper §5.4, Table 2, and §7](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=9)

### Placement: judge colocation and separation by the iteration's critical path

Section 6's auto-mapping searches model colocation, GPU allocation, and parallel strategies before training, estimating iteration latency with simulators. Within a stage, colocated computation times add, while independent resource groups contribute their maximum; stage latencies are then added. Figures 12–13 show that the favorable placement changes with cluster size and model combinations. Neither universal colocation nor universal separation follows from the paper. This is also not an online scheduler that rearranges resources after every tool call. [Paper §6, Algorithm 1, and §8.3](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=10)

### Read the experiment conditions before the speedups

The testbed has up to 16 machines and 128 A100-80GB GPUs, eight per machine. The paper reports 600 GB/s NVLink within a machine and 200 Gbps between machines. Experiments cover 7B–70B Llama models and PPO, ReMax, and Safe-RLHF. Main end-to-end comparisons use equally sized actor, reference, critic, and reward models; a separate placement experiment uses unequal model sizes. [Paper §8.1–8.3](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=10)

**Prompt and response lengths are each fixed at 1,024 tokens, with a global batch of 1,024; results average five iterations after ten warm-up iterations.** Throughput counts prompt plus response tokens divided by full RL iteration time. Equal response lengths are deliberately enforced to control differences in baseline continuous-batching support. [Paper §8.1](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=11)

The abstract's 1.53–20.57× range spans algorithms, scales, and baselines; it is not a uniform improvement over one contemporary best baseline. Compared versions are DeepSpeed-Chat 0.14.0, OpenRLHF 0.2.5, and NeMo-Aligner 0.2.0. Read Figures 9–11 for end-to-end throughput, Fig. 14 for transition time, and Fig. 15 for the joint cost of transition and generation. A local reduction is not automatically an equal iteration-level speedup.

Two further boundaries matter. The mapping algorithm assumes homogeneous GPUs and relies on estimated workloads and stage costs. Throughput results also do not establish faster convergence or higher final reward. Changed workloads, hardware, and baseline versions require new critical-path and training-quality measurements rather than reuse of the headline multiplier. [Paper §6 and §8](https://i.cs.hku.hk/~cwu/papers/gmsheng-eurosys25.pdf#page=10)

### Applying the ideas to rollout, refit, and agent tails

**The following is systems analysis, not an additional experiment reported by the paper.** Separate refit timing into four parts: training-weight export and layout conversion, transport, inference-engine loading, and cache or execution-state restoration. The 3D-HybridEngine addresses avoidable work in layout changes and data movement. Engine-specific loading, quantization-format conversion, and other restoration costs still need their own measurements.

Multi-turn agents introduce uneven trajectory lengths and completion times through tool waits. If updating requires a complete batch, a few slow trajectories can determine when the stage transition starts. Compare colocated switching costs with separated synchronization costs, while also recording tail latency, trainer waiting, useful trajectories per second, policy-version staleness, and task quality. **Fixed-length generation experiments do not demonstrate that these tail problems are solved.**

A concrete debugging sequence is to ask whether GPUs are waiting for the last trajectory, whether ready weights are blocked in engine loading, and whether the next requests repeat prefill because caches must be rebuilt. All three can appear as slow rollout, but they concern a batch barrier, the refit path, and cache validity respectively. Changing one TP setting is not a complete diagnosis.

### Current verl is a separate implementation layer to verify

Official documentation checked on 2026-10-15 places the current worker entry points in engine_workers. Its update_weights path distinguishes direct colocated updates from checkpoint-engine transport for separated execution. Historical class names and 3D group construction should therefore not be equated with every current backend. This section describes the documentation; it does not claim a particular configuration was executed or benchmarked. [Engine Workers](https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/workers/engine_workers.html)

Agent Loop separately defines multi-turn execution and response_mask, distinguishing generated tokens from tool-returned tokens. The V1 Async Trainer adds partial-trajectory resumption, policy-version spans, and staleness handling; resumption may rebuild prefix KV. These are developments after the paper, not results that can be retroactively attributed to its original evaluation. [Agent Loop](https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/advance/agent_loop.html); [V1 Async Trainer](https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/advance/v1_async_trainer.html)

### Reading order and three questions to answer

Start with Figures 1, 2, and 5 to draw control and data paths. Use Figures 7 and 8 to work through one weight-layout transition by hand, then inspect Table 2 and the evaluation conditions in §8. Answer three questions: where do control messages and large tensors travel; which weights does each GPU hold before and after the switch; and which terms are missing from the iteration cost model when rollout includes long-tailed tool execution?

## Optional reading · SGLang: Efficient Execution of Structured Language Model Programs

**Year / publication：** 2024 · NeurIPS 2024 · [Original source](<https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/724be4472168f31ba1c9ac630f15dec8-Abstract-Conference.html>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth a close read with code. Start with Full/SWA/Mamba reuse conditions and HiCache tiers, then trace matching and recovery through the tool-call example. Source checked on October 15, 2026 and pinned to commit f2b73d4.

### Why SGLang is today's source-code reading

**Worth a close read, starting with caching.** The original paper's RadixAttention organizes shared prefixes across calls in a radix tree and combines cache retention, eviction, and scheduling to reduce repeated prefill. It explains why reuse matters. Today's implementation reading concerns state management at commit `f2b73d4`; the 2024 paper is not a specification of the current code. [Original paper, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07104v2#S3)

### Code facts: separate two dimensions

First unpack the terminology: Full refers to historical KV for full-attention layers; SWA (Sliding Window Attention) needs KV for the relevant window; recurrent layers such as Mamba need state snapshots at particular positions. Their requirements for reusing history differ.

Full, SWA, and Mamba describe the state the model needs to retain; HiCache describes where that state lives. [registry.py](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/registry.py#L150-L199) composes Full with the required SWA/Mamba components in a unified tree, then optionally attaches HiCache. [Pure SWA has a separate selection branch](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/registry.py#L127-L130), so this is not the sole implementation path for every model.

Matching is more than finding the longest shared token prefix: the [tree core](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/unified_cache/unified_tree_core.py#L917-L970) requires all component validators to pass and separately tracks Full-KV hit length and the boundary from which execution can resume.

Full hit length describes coverage for one component. The overall resumable boundary must satisfy the other components as well. A suitable host-memory copy still has to reach the GPU before execution.

### A tool-call example: why 4,096 identical tokens may be insufficient

Suppose a coding agent calls a test tool after 4,096 tokens, then appends the returned result. Its model and input configuration are unchanged, and the old prefix is identical. The numbers are illustrative; all positions are aligned.

If the Full layers retain the entire prefix's KV, they have a basis for reusing 4,096 tokens. Now consider SWA with a 1,024-token window: if a page is missing from the final window, Full KV alone does not allow SWA to resume directly at 4,096. Execution needs a valid window boundary or recomputation of missing state.

This example is derived from the [SWA validator and result handling](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/unified_cache/components/swa.py#L355-L421): missing state resets contiguous coverage, and the Full hit can extend beyond the SWA boundary. A host copy can participate in matching but must be restored before use. This discussion covers tree-stored windows; the code also has a special case for per-request ring buffers.

If the model contains Mamba layers and retains a recurrent-state checkpoint only at 3,072, identical tokens do not make direct resumption at 4,096 possible. Execution must advance from a valid checkpoint. [Mamba validation and branching positions](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/unified_cache/components/mamba.py#L147-L190) require state at the node, and [splitting a node](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/unified_cache/components/mamba.py#L309-L319) does not invent state for the new boundary. A hybrid model must satisfy all its components; the longer Full hit can still help subsequent recovery.

### HiCache changes placement; recovery still has a cost

The [controller](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/hybrid_cache/hybrid_cache_controller.py#L964-L1035) queues host-to-device restoration and submits storage prefetches; its [storage queries](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/f2b73d4f9b4e64415b8d775b0f811c7801f1728b/python/sglang/srt/mem_cache/hybrid_cache/hybrid_cache_controller.py#L1348-L1412) also handle per-pool hit information. Consequently, “available in host memory” and “ready for GPU execution” need separate accounting. HiCache is not a fourth model-cache type alongside SWA and Mamba.

### Connection to your agent systems work

**My systems inference:** during a tool wait, a residency policy should ask which states enable inexpensive continuation, rather than counting retained tokens alone. Measure resumable positions, resident Full/SWA/recurrent state, transfer time, and recomputation time separately before comparing retention, offloading, and recomputation. Resumable state here covers the model side only; tool containers, session side effects, and training optimizer state need separate management.

### Suggested reading order

Start with §3 and Figure 3 of the paper for the motivation. Follow registry's component selection, the SWA/Mamba match validators, and finally the controller's load/prefetch paths. For a first pass, being able to explain the tool-return example is enough. This is valuable for refining the state model behind agent KV scheduling, but these files alone do not establish an optimal tool-wait policy.

## Optional reading · Ray: A Distributed Framework for Emerging AI Applications

**Year / publication：** 2018 · OSDI 2018 · [Original source](<https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/moritz>)

**Reading guidance：** Selectively read the task/actor model and scheduler. Explain why rollout workers often use persistent actors and where model state and large tensors should live.

### Why return to Ray after HybridFlow

HybridFlow expresses the algorithmic relationship between training and rollout. Ray supplies abstractions for coordinating remote computation and persistent workers. The 2018 paper combines tasks and actors in one execution framework with dynamic dependencies and distributed scheduling. Keep three questions separate: when the algorithm advances, which process executes, and how tensors move. [OSDI paper and presentation](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/moritz)

### What tasks, actors, and ObjectRefs represent

A task expresses a remote function computation. An actor is a persistent, stateful process whose methods can access and modify existing state. A rollout worker with expensive model initialization can use an actor to retain its initialized engine across requests. This process abstraction is different from the policy model called the actor in PPO. [Current actor documentation](https://docs.ray.io/en/latest/ray-core/actors.html)

An ObjectRef returned by a remote call is similar to a future. The caller can arrange dependent work without immediately fetching the result into the controller. Ray remote objects are immutable and may reside in object stores on different nodes; the location of a reference does not determine the location of the data. [Current object documentation](https://docs.ray.io/en/latest/ray-core/objects.html)

### A rollout debugging example: submitted work, idle GPU

**This is a diagnostic example, not a paper experiment.** Suppose a controller submits generate and will calculate rewards afterward. First check whether the worker has started and obtained its requested resources. Next check whether input dependencies are ready or data is moving between nodes. Only then check whether execution has reached the GPU. Calling all three delays “Ray overhead” hides different optimization problems.

Even a ready ObjectRef does not establish that rollout used the intended policy version. Tag each batch with its policy version and separately record request submission, dependency readiness, first GPU work, batch completion, and installation of new weights. These observations help distinguish control overhead, transfer, computation, and version transitions. This is an application-level instrumentation proposal to implement and validate in your own system.

### Half a GPU resource does not reserve half its memory

Current Ray documentation defines resource quantities as logical admission controls. `num_gpus=0.5` can allow actors to share a visible GPU, but does not partition device memory into isolated halves. Ray sets `CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES`; the application still manages the real footprint of models, KV state, temporary tensors, and communication buffers. [Resources and fractional GPUs](https://docs.ray.io/en/latest/ray-core/scheduling/resources.html)

**My systems inference:** when a trainer and a rollout engine alternate on the same GPUs, resource declarations solve only part of placement. Releasing KV, offloading optimizer state, establishing communication groups, and completing weight installation still require a protocol between the training framework and inference engine. Declaring each process to require half a GPU does not establish safe or efficient coexistence.

### Reading scope and connection to current work

Read the task/actor model, dynamic dependencies, and scheduler selectively; detailed fault-tolerance internals can wait. Processes, remote calls, and futures are enough background. Then draw your worker graph: which states remain inside actors, which batches flow through references, and which model communications use dedicated paths?

Use the paper for the 2018 design and the official documentation, checked on October 15, 2026, for the API behavior described above. Task throughput in the paper is not a prediction of LLM rollout throughput. Measure scheduling, transfer, and computation in your own workflow. The site's [[EN/wiki/rl-infra/rl-training-frameworks|RL training frameworks]] note can help place Ray beneath algorithm frameworks.

## Optional reading · DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized Large Language Model Serving

**Year / publication：** 2024 · OSDI 2024 · [Original source](<https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/zhong-yinmin>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth a close read after the main paper. Distinguish throughput from goodput, then examine the benefit of independent configurations and the cost of KV transfer.

### What comes after continuous batching

Orca explains iteration-level batch membership, while PagedAttention explains efficient KV memory use. DistServe asks whether prefill and decode should always share GPUs and parallel configurations. It separates the phases and chooses resources and parallelism around time to first token (TTFT) and average time per subsequent output token (TPOT). [OSDI 2024 paper page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/zhong-yinmin)

### High throughput can still miss the service target

Consider a hypothetical interference scenario: users are waiting for streaming output when a new long prompt arrives. Processing it on the same GPUs might increase aggregate work while delaying requests already decoding. Users care about prompt first output and smooth continuation, which total tokens/s alone cannot express. This is a motivating example; the actual effect depends on batching and kernel behavior.

DistServe evaluates the arrival rate supportable at a chosen SLO attainment target and compares capacity per GPU. Fix latency requirements and the attainment target before reading throughput comparisons; relaxed requirements can otherwise create the appearance of greater capacity. [Paper, §6.1–6.2](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi24-zhong-yinmin.pdf#page=10)

### Independent configurations come with a KV transfer bill

Separation permits different parallel configurations and instance counts, but prefill must hand KV state to decode. Placement accounts for connectivity: when inter-node bandwidth is limited, corresponding stages share a machine to use faster local links. Simply putting the phases on two machines omits this condition. [Paper, §3–4](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi24-zhong-yinmin.pdf#page=4)

**An illustrative bandwidth lower bound:** if every request transfers 1 GiB of KV and arrivals average eight requests per second, KV traffic alone is about 8 GiB/s, before protocol overhead, contention, or other communication. Below that available bandwidth, queues grow; slightly exceeding it still does not guarantee acceptable tail latency. This is a capacity-planning example, not the paper's measured configuration.

### Reading the evidence and its limits

The physical testbed has four nodes with 32 A100 80GB GPUs, intra-node NVLink, and 25 Gbps inter-node connectivity. Workloads use OPT models for chat, code completion, and summarization. High-bandwidth comparisons in §6.4 include simulation. Table 1, Figure 7, and Figure 11 expose the SLOs, length distributions, and ablation conditions. [Experiments and ablations](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi24-zhong-yinmin.pdf#page=10)

**My systems inference:** for rollout, draw the entire trajectory timeline: model computation, tool waiting, KV recovery, reward completion, and formation of a training batch. Better online TTFT/TPOT need not finish the last trajectory earlier; tools or batch barriers may dominate the tail. Compare phase latency, traffic, GPU utilization, and whole-batch trajectory completion together.

### Reading recommendation and next connection

Worth a close read. For today, follow the causal chain: different phase requirements allow independent configurations, at the cost of state transfer. Orca and PagedAttention provide enough background once TTFT/TPOT are clear. Read §2 motivation, §3 cost analysis, §4 placement, then §6 evaluation.

Then ask whether your workload is constrained by prefill, decode, networking, or tool tails. That answer determines what to measure and whether separation is promising; the paper's maximum speedup alone cannot decide. Compare the site's [[EN/wiki/llm-serving-for-agents/infercept|InferCept]] note to distinguish phase isolation from KV management during tool waits.
