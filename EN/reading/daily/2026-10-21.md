# What has a model learned when its answers improve?

When accuracy improves, what has a model learned, and what changes simply make existing capabilities easier to express? The main reading is an October study of token cues, moving from fixing two opening tokens to causal interventions on training data. Two optional readings examine mode switching in hybrid reasoning models and the internal features changed by RL and supervised fine-tuning. All three use controlled comparisons, but their subjects and claims differ; none establishes that all reinforcement learning adds no new capability. Start with the concrete example and return to representations and interventions on a second pass.

Each digest recommends 1 main paper and 2–4 optional papers across LLM inference, RL infrastructure, post-training, RSI (recursive self-improvement), reasoning compute, agent learning and memory, data and evaluation, and model architecture. Explore foundations and current research by theme; a direct connection to current work is not required. A recommendation does not mean it has been read, and there is no need to finish everything in one day.

## Main reading · Base Models Can Reason By Taking a Cue From Training Data

**Year / publication：** 2026 · arXiv 2026 · MIT / UC Berkeley / UW / Ai2 · v1 2026-10-05 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06851v1>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth a close read. Start with Figures 1–5 and §2–3, then check Table 6, Table 16, and Appendices B/F for sampling and intervention conditions. The main lesson is how the authors test the explanation that a behavior is already available but not usually expressed, rather than memorizing a useful word.

### The same problem, a supplied opening, and a different continuation

The paper shows a problem in which 834 students take music and represent two-thirds of a school's students. The correct calculation is 834 × 3 / 2 = 1251. Using the same Olmo-3-7B base model, the authors supply different response openings and let it continue. One Answer-opening response immediately gives the incorrect 1002. An Okay-opening response writes down the relationship, calculates 1251, and checks the calculation. This is a concrete example from the paper, not an outcome guaranteed on every generation.

The opening does not contain the correct answer, and the model is not trained between those generations. The intervention supplies a very short response prefix, called a prefill. This permits a more specific question: could the model not solve the problem, or was its default generation more likely to choose another way of responding? A single example cannot settle this, so the paper follows it with evaluations across problems, RL comparisons, and training-data interventions.

This note reads the October 5, 2026 v1 paper, whose authors Sophie L. Wang, Amil Dravid, and colleagues are affiliated with MIT, UC Berkeley, the University of Washington, and the Allen Institute for AI. It is licensed CC BY 4.0. Its main contribution is the sequence of controls: the authors do not merely find an effective prompt, but return to the training data to investigate why it works. [Paper v1 and Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06851v1) · [Authors' project page](https://www.sophielwang.com/cues)

### How often an opening appears differs from what follows when it is given

The effective Olmo prefix is written `.\n\nOkay`, where `\n` denotes a newline rather than a literal backslash and letter n. For this model's tokenizer, the period and two newlines form one token, and Okay forms another. Tokens are the text units processed by the model; they are not necessarily words. The authors call a short prefix that influences subsequent behavior a token cue.

For a fixed problem, the probability of a complete answer decomposes into `probability of the opening × probability of the continuation given that opening`. These factors have different roles. A model may rarely produce an opening by itself yet continue with a good solution when it receives that opening. Forced prefilling bypasses the first choice and lets us inspect the conditional continuation; it does not directly modify the weights.

A base model need not be unfamiliar with reasoning text. The Olmo and other models here have already encountered such material during pretraining and subsequent mid-training, before the RL updates being compared. The original evaluation prompts also request step-by-step solutions. The precise intervention is therefore to retain the problem and instructions, change only the response opening, and examine the additional effect. [§2 and Appendix B.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S2)

### Finding a cue through answer agreement on training problems

The authors take 30 MATH training problems and use beam search to find openings the model is relatively likely to generate. Here beam search simply retains a set of high-probability candidates at each step: a width of 20 and a depth of two tokens produce 20 short openings. For every opening and problem, they then sample 16 complete responses and inspect how concentrated the final answers are.

The selection metric is answer entropy. When the 16 responses to a problem concentrate on fewer answers, entropy is lower. The authors select the candidate with the lowest average entropy without consulting reference answers. They also filter candidates whose missing-answer rate worsens substantially, preventing a consistently absent answer from looking attractive. Once selected, one cue is held fixed across all evaluation problems and benchmarks for that model–prompt pair. [§2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S2.SS1)

No answer labels does not mean no cost. Candidate scoring alone requires 20 × 30 × 16 = 9,600 continuations, calculated from the stated setup and excluding candidate search. Agreement can also mean that every response is wrong. It is an empirically supported selection proxy here, not a guarantee of correctness. A new distribution still needs independent evaluation; repeatedly choosing the best word on the test set would not provide the same evidence.

### How large is the gain? Separate one-attempt accuracy from repeated attempts

MATH-500 is a 500-problem mathematics test set. In Table 6, Olmo-3-7B under its fixed RL-Zero prompt improves from 42.2% without a prefill to 77.9% with one. Qwen3-14B under its own boxed prompt improves from 72.2% to 86.6%, using ` Alright,` with a leading space. These are comparisons within each model, not a cross-model ranking that treats the two prompt formats as identical.

Pass@1 is the probability that one sampled response is correct. The authors sample 32 responses per problem to estimate it; a problem does not count as solved merely because one of those 32 is correct. That would be a different repeated-attempt metric. Default sampling uses temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and a 31,744-token generation cap. Temperature and top-p control stochastic sampling, while the cap limits response length. Error bars capture variation across rollout indices, not independent training runs. [Appendix B.1 and Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#A2.SS1)

The same cue is reused on other mathematics benchmarks and HumanEval coding tests. Olmo improves in both mathematics and code; Qwen's coding results remain close to its already high baseline. Gains are not uniform across tasks. Olmo's MATH-500 pass@16—the probability of at least one correct response in 16 attempts—moves only from 94.0% to 95.4%, so the large one-attempt improvement should not be described as a doubling of the range of solvable problems.

### What RL changes: compare predictions after exactly the same history

If two models have already written different text, later differences may simply follow from their different contexts. The authors therefore supply identical token histories to the base and RL models and compare the next-token distributions at each position. They measure distributional discrepancy with KL divergence. Knowing the formula is not necessary here; its role is to reveal where the predictions differ.

On the tested trajectories, the largest differences occur at the first two positions and become much smaller afterward. The target opening's probability rises from 0.14 to 0.65 for Olmo and from 0.04 to 0.58 for Qwen. The authors also let the base model continue an RL-generated opening, recovering approximately RL-level performance. That swapping experiment switches models two tokens after the first blank line; it must not be described as exchanging strictly the first two tokens. [§2.3 and Appendix D.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S2.SS3)

Another experiment compares ordinary RL with RL that always supplies the cue. It uses GRPO, sampling several answers to a problem and updating from binary correctness rewards relative to the group's mean. The authors train LoRA for 300 steps, learning only a compact set of weight updates, with a 4,096-token training response cap, distinct from the evaluation budget above. Cue forcing starts at high accuracy before training and leaves less subsequent improvement. This supports the explanation that much of the gain in these runs comes from more often entering an existing continuation behavior, not a claim that all RL only learns openings.

### Could the improvement simply come from writing longer answers?

This alternative explanation needs a control. With the cue, Olmo's average response does grow from roughly 4,800 to 6,700 tokens. But some other candidate openings produce responses about twice as long as the effective cue while scoring below the no-cue baseline. More generated text alone cannot explain why particular openings work.

The authors also truncate already generated responses to the same token count and check whether an extractable correct answer appears within that prefix. The effective Olmo cue remains ahead of no cue from truncation lengths of roughly 1,000 tokens onward. This supports an advantage beyond length alone. It is not a fresh generation in which the model was told its budget in advance, nor a serving comparison at equal GPU time. [§2.4 and Appendix E.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S2.SS4)

The control has a boundary too: at very small truncation budgets, Qwen3-14B can do worse with the cue. A cue that encourages extended reasoning may be unsuitable when an application requires a very short answer. Comparing generation methods should therefore consider correctness, output length, and actual latency together. That last measurement is an application-oriented recommendation, not a result already measured in this paper.

### The causal intervention: replace Okay with Chicken in training data

The authors find that 83% of synthetic reasoning traces in Olmo's mid-training data start with Okay. That statistic alone does not establish the cause of its effect. They therefore restart mid-training from the same pretrained checkpoint, keeping the recipe, data order, and random seed fixed while editing words in the data. Mid-training continues learning on a particular data mixture after pretraining; it differs from RL driven by answer rewards.

One run uses the original text. A second replaces whole-word Okay/okay with Chicken/chicken throughout the mixture, called the rename condition. Both train for 10 billion tokens. On MATH-500 with a 7,168-token response cap, Chicken-cued pass@1 changes from 2.4% to 37.2%, close to Okay's 38.3% after renaming. These are shorter retraining experiments. Their values cannot be attached to the released base model's earlier 77.9% as though they describe one continuous trajectory. [Appendix F.1 and Table 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#A6.SS1)

Chicken need not mean 'think carefully' in ordinary language to become a reasoning cue in a particular context. What matters is the text it repeatedly precedes during training. However, no-cue accuracy also rises from 13.8% to 23.3% after renaming. We cannot claim that the data edit changes only one word's behavior and leaves everything else untouched; the causal evidence comes from the complete pattern across explicitly controlled conditions.

### Removing the old association is not enough: redirect Okay toward a new question

The rename experiment reveals an important distinction. Even when this mid-training text no longer contains Okay, forcibly supplying it still elicits reasoning. Its probability of appearing naturally decreases dramatically, while its conditional continuation effect persists. The authors note that the cue already works at an earlier pretrained checkpoint; older associations may explain why simply deleting the word from this stage does not erase its effect.

The third condition edits the rename data further, replacing paragraph-initial Question: in short question–answer data with Okay,. This is the redirect condition. Okay is now associated with a question coming next rather than a solution to the supplied problem. After training, Okay-cued MATH-500 accuracy falls to 0.2%, while Chicken remains at 40.1% and no cue scores 26.7%. The model has not globally lost its ability to solve problems; a particular opening's relationship with subsequent behavior has changed. [§3.1 and Table 16](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S3.SS1)

Supplementary experiments find similar patterns in SmolLM3. A separate full 100-billion-token Olmo rename run raises Chicken-cued accuracy from the unedited control's 18.2% to 76.9%. That is a different training budget and must not be mixed with the 10-billion-token comparison. These replications strengthen the data-association account, but both intervention models include synthetic reasoning traces in their training mixtures. They do not guarantee the same effect for arbitrary pretraining data and models.

### What the internal evidence supports—and what it does not establish

Hidden states are numerical vectors produced as a model processes text through its layers. The authors compare Olmo layer-24 vectors from generated responses with vectors produced by training documents at the same layer. They find the ten most similar neighbors and inspect which document categories those neighbors belong to. After an effective cue, response states resemble states from synthetic reasoning traces more closely. Openings such as Answer instead shift similarity toward short question–answer text.

This is correlational evidence connecting generation to types of training text. It does not mean the model retrieves those documents at runtime, and it does not identify an independent neural circuit controlling reasoning. The paper includes a useful counterexample: an opening can move states toward reasoning-related text while the model merely restates the task and lists steps without calculating an answer. A reasoning-like style does not establish correct reasoning. [§3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S3.SS2)

Position matters as well. Inserting Okay in the first or second paragraph produces a more sustained similarity shift and improves accuracy. Later insertion yields a brief shift and can perform worse than the baseline. Its effect therefore depends on the context already accumulated. It is not a word that can be inserted anywhere to restart correct reasoning.

### What to take away, and what to check during a close read

The most useful distinction is between what a model can do under appropriate conditions and how likely it is to express that behavior by default. Response prefills, RL comparisons, renaming, and redirection turn this distinction into experiments. They provide a concrete explanation for the tested settings, not a verdict on every complex post-R1 pipeline. The authors explicitly limit the comparison to an RL-Zero-style setting that starts directly from base models.

Other boundaries matter too. None of the tested openings yields an effective cue for Llama-3.1-8B, transfer across mathematics and code is uneven, and AIME performance in the short retraining experiments remains low. Sampling error bars do not substitute for checks across independently trained seeds. The paper deserves attention for its clear intervention design, not because it proves that two tokens replace modern RL. [§6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1#S6)

A useful reading order is the Figure 1 example, cue selection in §2.1, RL comparisons in §2.3, the three data conditions in §3.1, and internal evidence in §3.2. A reproduction should fix the model version, tokenizer, prompt template, prefix bytes, sampling settings, and evaluation budget, while separating cue-selection problems from evaluation problems. This is a recommendation distilled from the experimental design. Official code is available; this note has not rerun the model experiments. [Official code](https://github.com/sophicle/cues)

## Optional reading · The Thinking Leakage in Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

**Year / publication：** 2026 · arXiv preprint · v1 · 2026-10-09 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14270v1>)

**Reading guidance：** Read the bidirectional activation exchanges in §5.2 closely, then §5.3 to distinguish internal mechanism evidence from task performance; pair this with today's main reading on eliciting model capabilities.

### 1. Not displaying extended reasoning does not remove the ability

Imagine giving the same mathematical problem to the same model through two paths: one first writes a derivation, then answers; the other answers directly. This is a teaching illustration. The Qwen3 modes studied share weights—the model's stored parameters—and differ in their chat templates. THINK permits a derivation inside &lt;think&gt;, whereas NO-THINK appends an empty thinking block to the input: &lt;think&gt;, two newlines, &lt;/think&gt;, and two more newlines. This indicates that the thinking portion has already ended. Here, hybrid reasoning means two modes in one model; it differs from the attention/SSM hybrid architecture discussed in earlier readings. The paper's original model is a released reasoning checkpoint, not a purely pretrained model without post-training. See [§3: models and templates](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S3).

If training the second path improves accuracy while long derivations reappear, at least two explanations remain: the model learned new methods, or it became more willing to use an existing reasoning behavior. The authors call the latter thinking leakage. Leakage here refers to behavior reappearing across modes, not leaked test answers. Distinguishing the explanations requires a comparison with the original THINK mode and interventions on internal computation. See [§1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S1).

### 2. What training changes and how much evaluation permits

The authors train Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B on DAPO-Math-17k using the NO-THINK template throughout. Each step samples 256 problems and five responses per problem at temperature one. GRPO adjusts generation probabilities using reward differences among answers to the same problem. They train rank-64 LoRA adapters on every linear layer: learnable updates constrained to a low-rank structure, rather than directly training all original parameters. See [§3: training](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S3).

Evaluation covers 90 problems from AIME I and II in 2022–2024. The final boxed answer is graded, and a missing boxed answer counts as incorrect. Both modes allow 32,768 generated tokens; Figure 1 uses two samples per problem at temperature one. Training caps differ: 8k for 8B and 16k for the other sizes. Equal evaluation caps establish permission to generate equally long responses, not equal realized output lengths. See [§3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S3) and [Appendix H](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A8).

### 3. First observe: greater accuracy accompanies more THINK-like behavior

In Figure 1, 8B NO-THINK accuracy rises from approximately 17% to 43%, while mean response length increases from 2.7k to 7.2k tokens; the original THINK accuracy is approximately 74%. The authors also place the same response after each template and ask the original model to assign its likelihood. Trained responses favor the THINK template more strongly than original NO-THINK responses in approximately 94% of pairwise comparisons. This is neither 94% problem-solving accuracy nor human judgment. See [§4 and Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S4).

The temporary numerical vectors computed inside each layer are activations. Holding response text constant while changing the template or weights, the authors find that RL activations also move toward the original THINK state. This excludes the explanation that the measurement merely follows different text. Correlation is still insufficient, however: newly learned methods might produce similar activations. The next experiments actively change weights or activations and examine the resulting behavior. See [§3's measurement definitions](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S3) and [§5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5).

### 4. Scale the weight update to probe its direction

Write the original weights as W₀ and the learned change as ΔW. Without further training, the authors test W₀ + cΔW, moving farther along the learned update. For the 8B step-67 update at c = 3, NO-THINK accuracy reaches 72.2%, and approximately 50.6% of responses spontaneously emit &lt;/think&gt;, although the template already closed the thinking block. This points more specifically to returning mode behavior than merely counting words such as 'wait.' See [§5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS1) and [Appendix B, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A2).

The factors were explored on the same 90 problems, with two samples per problem; the selected setting was not confirmed on a fresh test set. Larger factors can produce excessively long responses that reach the cap. No tested factor exceeded the original THINK accuracy of approximately 74%, but this observation neither establishes a theoretical ability ceiling nor shows that scaling weights generally beats further training. See [Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A2).

### 5. Exchange activations: can the gain be removed or transferred?

A stronger test exchanges only a small component of the activations. Using the original model and 128 training problems, the authors identify eight directions per layer associated with the mode difference: an eight-dimensional subspace. During generation, both models use the NO-THINK template and read exactly the same tokens. At layers 1–35 and every position, the source model supplies the target's component within this subspace; other components remain unchanged. The dimension and layer range were fixed in advance, rather than selected using AIME results. See [§5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS2) and [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A3).

This independently sampled experiment uses 8B at step 67, two samples for each of 90 problems, and a 32k cap. Unpatched accuracy is 18.9% for the original and 41.1% for the RL model, a 22.2-percentage-point gain. Replacing the RL model's THINK-subspace component with the original model's reduces accuracy to 24.4%, removing approximately 75% of that gain. The reverse exchange raises original-model accuracy to 30.0%, transferring approximately 50% of the gain. These percentages are not fractions of all reasoning ability. See [§5.2, Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS2) and [Appendix C, Table 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A3).

Exchanging an equally sized PCA subspace—the directions of greatest variation in original activations—removes only approximately 22% of the gain and transfers approximately 25%; random directions have smaller effects. Arbitrarily disturbing a component therefore does not reproduce the same result. However, patching also changes response lengths and does not remove the entire improvement. It supports an important role for this particular mode mechanism without completely explaining every change made by RL. See [§5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS2).

### 6. Which attention heads carry the template signal?

An attention head can be understood as one computational channel for reading preceding information. The authors identify heads that strongly attend to the empty think block and prevent those heads from reading its template tokens, without changing other inputs. In 8B, intervening on the top 5% of heads reduces the next-token distribution's KL divergence from THINK by 72%, versus an average 3% for five random selections of equally many heads. KL here measures a difference between probability distributions; 72% is not an accuracy figure. See [§5.3, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS3).

These experiments process the first 2,048 tokens of fixed responses without freely generating new answers. Single-head exchanges further show that heads transmitting the strongest RL-induced activation changes also attend strongly to the empty block. Heads are ranked using 30 texts and checked on another 60. This adds evidence about the channels through which templates affect internal states; it does not show that deleting these heads improves problem-solving accuracy, and individual head effects cannot simply be added. See [§5.3, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS3) and [Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A5).

### 7. Why might the reward push in this direction?

A GRPO advantage expresses how a response compares with its group and influences the update strength. For example, the sole correct answer among five receives a stronger positive signal than a correct answer in a group where four succeed. The authors separately sample 20 answers to each of 256 training problems and observe that rewarded responses are more THINK-like. A controlled gradient update computed from the initial model also moves its internal state toward THINK. This suggests a possible mechanism, not an inevitable property of every GRPO run. See [§6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S6) and [Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A7).

For evaluation work, report the template, original THINK baseline, generated length, and intervention evidence alongside the improvement. This study covers mathematics with three Qwen3 sizes; its central accuracy exchanges use only 8B. The paper does not report complete repetitions across multiple training seeds or accuracy confidence intervals. It cannot establish that RL never learns new abilities. This is a 2026-10-09 preprint by Wenxuan Zhu and collaborators at KAUST and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. This note adapts v1 into Chinese explanations and English paraphrases; the original is licensed under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Prioritize [§5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#S5.SS2) and [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14270v1#A3), followed by §5.3 and §6.

## Optional reading · Why Does Reinforcement Learning Generalize? A Feature-Level Mechanistic Study of Post-Training in Large Language Models

**Year / publication：** 2026 · ACL 2026 · Long Papers · July 2026 · [Original source](<https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1808/>)

**Reading guidance：** Study feature selection and intervention in §3.2 and §7, then check training differences and evaluation scope in Appendices A and B.4; the complete collection of feature-distribution plots can wait.

### 1. Why might mathematics training also improve science answers?

Consider a teaching scenario: a model continues training only on mathematics but subsequently becomes more likely to answer a science multiple-choice question such as which material conducts electricity. It might be using existing knowledge more effectively, or new internal computations might be involved. Final answers alone do not distinguish these explanations. This paper looks inside the model for features participating in changed answers. Generalization here concerns performance outside the current mathematics post-training domain, not proof that test knowledge was absent from pretraining.

Today's main reading examines how training-data cues in a prompt elicit base-model performance. This paper instead updates the weights and then modifies internal activity to observe whether answers change. Together they help investigate how existing capabilities are accessed, but neither alone establishes that all RL merely reorders old answers or necessarily creates entirely new capabilities.

### 2. The questions match, but the training answers differ

The authors start separately from Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen2.5-7B, using the same pool of approximately 47K mathematics problems. Supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, learns complete reasoning traces generated by Qwen3-32B-Instruct and filtered for correct final answers. RL uses GRPO: the model generates eight responses per question, receives rewards for final-answer correctness, and updates by comparing responses within the group. It does not see the teacher’s intermediate solution steps.

Both methods update all parameters, train for one epoch, and evaluate the final checkpoint, but this is not a compute-matched experiment. RL uses a learning rate of 1e−6, versus 5e−5 for SFT, and permits generations up to 16K tokens. Training uses H100/H200 GPUs. Matching initialization and question sources removes some differences without fixing supervision text, generation volume, or optimization settings, so the observations cannot be attributed to one isolated variable. See [Appendices A.2–A.3](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1808.pdf#page=13).

In the complete evaluations in Table 1, Qwen3 achieves 78.5% on SciQ science questions before training, 51.8% after SFT, and 89.5% after RL. However, Qwen2.5 drops from a base score of 77.6% to 76.1% after RL on CommonsenseQA. These results demonstrate cross-domain gains for this recipe while warning against reading “RL beats this SFT setup” as “RL beats the original model on every task.”

### 3. Build a common coordinate system for different models

As a model processes a token, it produces an intermediate numerical vector called an activation. Comparing individual vector coordinates does not readily reveal what they represent. The authors train a Sparse Crosscoder: it jointly encodes activations from different models into shared numerical coordinates, then reconstructs each original vector through that model’s decoder. Training encourages accurate reconstruction while activating only a small number of coordinates at a time. These shared coordinates are the sparse features here, rather than already identified “logic neurons.”

The analysis tool is trained on a separate 400 million tokens, half from OpenThoughts-114K and half from RedPajama general text; these are not the 47K-problem RL training inputs. It has 32,768 features and reconstructs middle-layer activations. For the joint comparison of base, SFT, and RL models, all three also share one Crosscoder, avoiding the mistake of treating coincident feature indices in separately trained tools as the same feature.

The plotted NRN and MAS measurements use decoder-vector magnitudes to assess which model a feature favors when reconstructing activations. Under this recipe, RL retains more components shared with the base model, whereas SFT has more strongly model-specific components. These are relative distributions inside an analysis tool, not percentages of reasoning capability preserved, and they do not mean RL produces no new features. See [§3.1, §5.1, and §6](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1808.pdf#page=3).

### 4. Find candidate features through answering differences

The authors next select questions that the base model gets wrong and the RL model gets right, separately for five general question-answering tasks. For these questions, they take final-token-position activations, encode them through the respective model branches, and calculate how much higher each feature’s activation is in RL on average. This identifies candidate changes that accompany improved answers; it has not yet established that those changes cause improvement.

Within each task they retain features scoring above 20% of the maximum score, then intersect the five task-specific sets. This yields 50 candidate features for Qwen3 and 16 for Qwen2.5. The intersection keeps candidates from depending on only one task type, but sample selection already uses knowledge of which model answered correctly. That fact determines how to interpret the numbers that follow. See [§3.2 and §7](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1808.pdf#page=4).

### 5. Do answers change when the features are modified?

The authors perform two inference-time interventions: zero the candidate feature values in the RL model, and set the corresponding base-model values to 3.0. The latter sets a value rather than multiplying the original by three. These operations modify intermediate activations without retraining the model weights, testing whether the candidates participate in producing answers. RL performance declines after zeroing, and base performance improves after setting the values, providing stronger intervention evidence than observing activations increase together.

The easily missed detail is the denominator: Tables 2–3 still evaluate base-wrong/RL-correct samples. For example, the −46.2 reported for Qwen3 on OpenBookQA means approximately 46.2% of those selected questions, originally answered correctly by RL, become wrong after zeroing. It is not a 46.2-percentage-point drop on the complete OpenBookQA test set. The main experiment also does not explicitly describe disjoint sample sets for feature discovery and intervention validation, so it should not be treated as an unbiased performance estimate on a fresh test set.

### 6. Transfer to other tasks, with explicit boundaries

Appendix B.4 applies the same features to LogiQA logical reasoning and PIQA physical commonsense questions. Neither task participated in feature selection, and interventions still affect answers. However, Tables 4–5 again evaluate only each task’s base-wrong/RL-correct subset. “Unseen tasks” means unseen during feature selection, not proven absence of their knowledge from pretraining, and these tables do not report gains across the complete tasks. See [Appendix B.4](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1808.pdf#page=15).

A defensible conclusion is therefore that, in these models, training recipes, and selected questions, a small group of jointly representable features participates in changed answers. The Crosscoder approximately reconstructs one intermediate layer rather than all computation. Changing a feature can change an answer without establishing that it corresponds to a particular human reasoning concept. Turning this into a training method would require independent questions, additional recipes, and complete performance evaluations.

### 7. A reading route and implications for experiments

Start with Table 1 and Appendix A to understand the actual training differences, then follow the question- and feature-selection steps in §3.2, and finally study interventions in §7 and Appendix B.4. Return to the equations in §3.1 after understanding that evidence chain. For systems experiments, one useful takeaway is to keep two reports: complete evaluations answer whether overall performance improved, while diagnostics on selected questions investigate which changes might participate. This is an experimental recommendation prompted by the paper, not a deployment method it has validated.
