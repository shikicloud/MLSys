# Test-time compute: sampling, verification, and budget allocation

The main ICLR 2025 reading explains sequential revision, parallel sampling, verifier search, and how difficulty and compute accounting shape the conclusions. Optional readings on Self-Consistency, Let’s Verify Step by Step, and STaR develop voting, process supervision, and training-time bootstrapping. Concrete examples distinguish spending more compute on one request from learning to solve future requests better. Start with Self-Consistency if these concepts are new.

Each digest recommends 1 main paper and 2–4 optional papers across LLM inference, RL infrastructure, post-training, RSI (recursive self-improvement), reasoning compute, agent learning and memory, data and evaluation, and model architecture. Explore foundations and current research by theme; a direct connection to current work is not required. A recommendation does not mean it has been read, and there is no need to finish everything in one day.

## Main reading · Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Can be More Effective than Scaling Parameters for Reasoning

**Year / publication：** 2025 · ICLR 2025 · preprint 2024 · [Original source](<https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf>)

**Reading guidance：** Main reading for this edition; worth studying closely. Start with the two axes and the 16-attempt example, examine the difficulty and budget curves in Figures 3, 6, and 7, then audit the FLOPs assumptions in Section 7. Allow 40–60 minutes.

### Start with a concrete question: where should extra computation go?

Snell, Lee, Xu, and Kumar ask how a fixed model should use additional inference budget on a particular problem: try again independently, revise an existing answer, or search through intermediate steps? The contribution is an analysis of when these choices help, rather than a new search algorithm that dominates on every problem. [Official ICLR 2025 record](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Abstract-Conference.html)

The 2024 preprint title ends with Scaling Model Parameters; the conference title ends with Scaling Parameters for Reasoning. This note follows the conference figures and compute accounting. Keep one distinction in mind throughout: making generation encounter a correct answer more often and reliably selecting that answer from the candidates are separate problems. [Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314); [conference Section 2](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=3)

### Two axes: change where candidates come from, or how they are selected

The proposal distribution assigns probabilities to possible answers under the model's current conditioning. Independent resampling mostly buys additional attempts. Conditioning on previous answers and asking for revisions changes the distribution used for the next attempt. In these experiments, test-time changes concern inputs and candidate distributions: revision capability was finetuned beforehand, and weights are not updated separately for each test question. [Conference Sections 2–3](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=3)

The second axis is the verifier. It can rank completed solutions after generation, or score partial solutions to guide pruning and expansion. The former spends computation on covering possible approaches; the latter tries to stop unpromising branches earlier. These axes can be combined, but the paper's revision-plus-answer-selection experiments should not be mistaken for a joint optimization of revisions and PRM tree search.

### Illustrative example: 16 attempts create three different execution shapes

**This is a teaching example, not a measured result from the paper.** Suppose the budget permits 16 complete attempts. A 16×1 allocation uses 16 independent starts; 1×16 uses one chain containing an initial answer and subsequent revisions; 4×4 uses four starts with four consecutive attempts in each chain. For now, count generations only. Answer lengths, historical context, and verifier costs will need separate accounting.

Imagine a geometry problem that admits either an auxiliary-line construction or a coordinate solution. A fully sequential chain might remain stuck with an unsuitable approach, while independent starts can cover alternatives. If the approach is sound and only one algebraic operation is wrong, revising may be more useful than starting over. This is an intuition for the results, not a guarantee that revisions are purely local. Answers must still be selected from the chain: its last output is not automatically its best. [Conference Figures 5 and 7](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=7)

### The PRM predicts continuation success; it is not a proof checker

The authors continue sampling from intermediate solution steps, estimate a prefix's value from the fraction of continuations that finish correctly, and train the PRM on these soft labels. Appendix F uses 16 solutions per training question and 16 Monte Carlo continuations per step. A score therefore estimates the chance of eventual success under the base model's continuation policy. It is not a certificate that the current mathematical step is logically valid. [Conference Section 5.1 and Appendix F](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=19)

A completed solution is scored using the PRM's final-step prediction. Scores are then summed across solutions sharing the same final answer, and the answer with the greatest sum wins: best-of-N weighted. This differs from choosing the single highest-scoring solution and from equally weighted majority voting. **As an illustrative aggregation example**, two solutions ending in 12 with scores of 0.6 each outweigh one ending in 10 with a score of 0.9. [Appendix G](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=20)

### Why more aggressive stepwise search can become worse

At each round, beam search scores partial solutions, retains promising prefixes, and branches from them to replenish the candidate pool. Lookahead adds a simulation: continue up to k steps and use the PRM score at that future prefix to evaluate the present choice. Better-informed selection is possible, but those trial continuations cost computation. The paper approximates this generation cost as N(k+1), rather than continuing to charge only N. [Conference Figure 2, Sections 5.2–5.3, and Appendix C](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=5)

In Figure 3, beam search helps at smaller budgets, but its advantage shrinks and sometimes reverses as the budget grows. The authors observe repetitive low-information steps and excessively short solutions, suggesting that search can exploit imperfections in the learned score. Optimizing a score harder does not necessarily improve actual correctness. Likewise, the reported lookahead results do not establish that every form of tree search is ineffective.

### Where revision ability comes from, and why selection remains necessary

Revision training is more than adding a request to think again. The authors sample 64 responses per question in parallel, place up to four incorrect responses in context, and supervise a correct response as the target. An edit-distance heuristic helps choose a related incorrect answer, encouraging the model to use previous attempts. At inference, retaining only the latest four attempts allows the model to run longer revision chains. [Conference Section 6.1 and Appendix L](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=24)

Training contexts primarily contain mistakes, whereas an inference-time answer may already be correct. Continuing to revise introduces a distribution shift. In this setup, roughly 38% of correct answers are changed into incorrect ones, motivating majority or verifier-based selection. The revision experiments use an ORM adapted to revision-model outputs, selecting within each chain and then aggregating across chains. This is distinct from the earlier PRM-guided search. [Conference Section 6.1 and Appendices M–N](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=7)

### Compute-optimal means conditioning the choice; estimating that condition is hard

Difficulty is relative to the model, rather than an intrinsic problem label. The oracle version estimates pass@1 from 2,048 samples per question and forms five difficulty bins. The predicted version instead averages verifier scores over those candidates, avoiding ground-truth answer labels. Difficulty and the available budget then determine the search method or sequential/parallel ratio. This is an empirical approximation to an ideal strategy, not a global optimality theorem or an implemented scheduler allocating resources across a multi-user queue. [Conference Section 3](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=4)

With verifier-based revision selection, easier questions favor more sequential computation, while harder ones benefit from balancing independent starts and subsequent revisions. **The major omission is that generating and scoring the 2,048 difficulty-estimation samples is excluded from the main compute comparison.** Predicted difficulty removes the dependency on true labels, but does not supply a cheap online router. A deployment must check whether estimating difficulty costs more than the inference computation it saves. [Conference Figure 7 and Appendix E](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=18)

### The experiments and 4×: separate answer counts from adjusted budgets

The main experiments use PaLM 2-S* (Codey) and a MATH split with 12,000 training and 500 test questions. Search uses a four-example prompt; the PRM and revision model are separately finetuned. This is not a demonstration that an arbitrary base model improves simply by being put in a loop. The conference version explicitly approximates verifier overhead as doubling computation, so plotted generation budgets should not automatically be read as counts of completed candidate answers. [Conference Sections 4–6 and Appendix J](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=5)

For revisions, 4× describes exceeding parallel best-of-N near selected budgets using less computation. Figure 6's caption gives 128 versus 512; Section 6.2 gives 64 versus 256. Their factor-of-two difference is consistent with charging for the verifier. This is an interpretation of two counting conventions; the prose does not harmonize its terminology. For search, Figure 4 describes approximately matching the baseline with roughly one-quarter of the budget. Neither result means four times the accuracy or four times measured speed, and neither includes the difficulty-estimation cost above. [Conference Figures 4 and 6, Section 6.2](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=8)

### The 14× larger model comparison: deployment volume changes the arithmetic

Section 7 matches total pretraining plus expected inference FLOPs, holding pretraining tokens fixed while increasing parameters. Let P denote parameters, Dpre training tokens, and Dinf inference tokens. The conference convention is X=6P·Dpre and Y=4P·Dinf, with the latter including approximate verifier overhead. Increasing parameters by M yields a budget of M(X+Y). Keeping the smaller model's training unchanged allows its inference budget to grow by F=M+1.5(M−1)/R, where R=Dinf/Dpre. [Conference Section 7](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=10)

**For an algebraic illustration**, M=14 and R=1 give F=33.5; this is not an additional measured point. At fixed average response length, more deployment queries increase Dinf and R, amortizing a one-time pretraining investment over more use. The paper evaluates R=0.08, 0.40, and 11. Easier questions or lower deployment volume more often favor additional small-model inference; hard questions and greater volume often favor the larger model. Figure 8 uses oracle difficulty and a greedy large-model baseline. It does not establish that any small model can beat one with 14 times as many parameters.

### The systems interpretation: minimizing FLOPs does not guarantee the fastest response

**The following is systems reasoning, not a GPU measurement from the paper.** Return to the 16-attempt example: independent branches may be batched together, whereas each revision must wait for its predecessor. Equal generated-token totals can therefore have different critical paths. Parallel branches also require more active KV state, while sequential revisions must handle carried-forward historical context. Actual prefix sharing and context changes determine how much caching can save.

Verifier placement, batching, queueing, and synchronization for intermediate scores also affect latency and utilization. A serving evaluation should jointly measure accuracy, generation and verification tokens, GPU time, end-to-end p50/p95 latency, and peak memory, then compare cost at matched quality. Extending the workload to tool-using agents additionally requires external waiting and recoverable execution state. These stages were not measured in the mathematical reasoning experiments.

### A reading route that turns the claims into checks

Begin with Sections 2–3 and Figure 5, drawing how candidates are generated and selected. Next read Figures 3, 6, and 7 to see whether different difficulty groups support one universal strategy. Finish with Section 7 and Appendices E/G/M to audit missing costs and aggregation rules. For every curve, ask what supplies the score, whether extra computation buys independent exploration or sequential depth, and whether its horizontal axis includes verification and routing costs.

This is a useful foundation for reasoning research, but its evidence centers on verifiable mathematics and does not automatically extend to open-ended research tasks. Distilling better inference outputs back into the model and repeating the cycle is a future direction in Appendix A, rather than a demonstrated sustained recursive self-improvement process. When reading later RSI work, look separately for evidence of better inference, weight updates, and net gains across multiple rounds. [Conference Appendix A](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1b623663fd9b874366f3ce019fdfdd44-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=16)

## Optional reading · Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models

**Year / publication：** 2023 · ICLR 2023 · preprint 2022 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth a close read of the method and budget curves, about 25–35 minutes. Start with §2 and Fig. 1, then the setup in §3.1–3.2 and Fig. 2, and finish with the consistency analysis in §3.5.

### Two-minute overview: turn one answer into multiple attempts

Self-Consistency is an [ICLR 2023 paper](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/poster/11718), first released as a 2022 preprint. It gives a fixed model several attempts and aggregates their answers. It neither updates the weights nor trains an additional verifier, a model that judges candidates. This makes it a useful first reading on inference-time computation: locate the extra work before asking whether the gain justifies it.

### Mechanism: sample reasoning paths and count final answers

Using the same prompt containing worked reasoning examples, sample K outputs, each containing a reasoning path and a final answer. Extract the answers, count their occurrences, and choose the most frequent. Although commonly called majority voting, the winner need not receive more than half the votes. Different calculations can count toward the same normalized answer. [Paper §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.11171#S2)

The aggregation operates on answers. It does not simply select the long output with the highest token likelihood, nor does it arrange a debate between models. Probability weighting is another option discussed in the paper; the basic version counts votes. An implementation needs explicit rules for answer formats, equivalent answers, and extraction failures because these affect the counts.

### Teaching example: three correct votes do not imply 60% confidence

**Teaching example, not a paper experiment:** A question asks for the number of pencils in three boxes of four, plus two loose pencils. Five samples answer 14, 10, 14, 12, and 14, so aggregation selects 14. The three successful paths might use multiplication, repeated addition, or combine two boxes first; the implementation only needs to extract the same final quantity.

The fraction 3/5 describes these samples, not a calibrated 60% probability of correctness. One model can repeatedly make the same mistake, including a unanimous wrong answer. The paper observes an empirical relationship between consistency and accuracy. Using that relationship to abstain or stop sampling still requires evaluation on the target task; a vote count is not automatically calibrated confidence.

### Systems connection: what multiple branches cost

**Systems analysis:** Expanding a prompt into K generation requests creates batching opportunities. An engine with prefix sharing may reuse KV for the identical prefix. Once branches generate different tokens, their subsequent KV and decode work remain necessary. This is an implementation implication, not a cache-speedup result measured by this paper.

Compare accuracy alongside total output tokens, peak memory, and request completion latency. K parallel requests neither cost nothing nor necessarily take K times the wall-clock time. Unequal branch lengths create tail waiting that can determine when a vote becomes available. Under a finite budget, compare several K values rather than presenting only the highest accuracy.

A minimal reproduction can fix the evaluation questions, prompts, model, and answer parser while varying the sample count. Record both single-response and aggregated accuracy, with extraction failures, ties, and timeouts reported separately. Early stopping is a different strategy that needs its own comparison: it changes which candidates can participate as well as reducing work. Gold test answers are unavailable during online inference; use a held-out offline evaluation set to measure accuracy.

### Evidence and limits: keep the sampling budget attached to the gain

The paper runs few-shot experiments with UL2, LaMDA, PaLM, and GPT-3 variants. Main results average ten independent runs of 40 samples against greedy CoT. On GSM8K, PaLM-540B rises from 56.5% to 74.4%, a 17.9-percentage-point gain, with temperature 0.7, top-k 40, and eight arithmetic exemplars. [Paper §3.1–3.2, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.11171#S3)

This does not show a benefit for every question or compare equal token budgets. Final answers must be meaningfully grouped: free-form writing, code, or tasks with several valid solutions cannot simply use raw-string voting. If correct answers are rarely produced, more samples may still fail; repeatedly sampling similar errors does not create independent evidence.

### Reading path: count one vote before inspecting the budget curve

Start with three concepts: greedy decoding selects the highest-probability token at each step; temperature sampling permits varied outputs; CoT prompts demonstrate reasoning followed by an answer. Then hand-count the votes using §2 and Fig. 1, inspect varying sample counts in Fig. 2, and finish with §3.5. Ask how many extra tokens doubling K consumes, and how to test whether concentrated votes are more reliable. These questions connect serving cost to answer quality.

## Optional reading · Let's Verify Step by Step

**Year / publication：** 2024 · ICLR 2024 · preprint 2023 · OpenAI · [Original source](<https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf>)

**Reading guidance：** Read §2.1, §2.4–2.6, Figures 3–4, and Appendices C/F closely. Establish the supervised object and evaluation budget before interpreting scores.

### The trained object is the judge, not an RL-updated generator

The [ICLR 2024 paper](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf) studies reliable reward models. A generator produces candidate solutions, a reward model scores them, and one is selected. Section 2.1 explicitly excludes reinforcement learning of the generator. Process supervision here is therefore a training signal for the judge; it does not establish that an RL policy has learned to reason correctly at every step.

This separates two failures: selection cannot recover a correct solution that never appears, whereas choosing incorrectly when a correct candidate exists points toward evaluation and ranking. **Our reading inference:** before increasing N, inspect both candidate coverage and selected-answer accuracy. Candidate count alone cannot identify where additional compute would be useful.

### ORM and PRM differ in the granularity of supervision

An outcome-supervised reward model (ORM) learns from final-result correctness. It can still read the entire solution; outcome supervision does not mean reading only the last number. A process-supervised reward model (PRM) learns step acceptability. Human feedback in this work uses positive, negative, and neutral labels, stopping at the first error. Step labels locate a failure; an outcome label usually says only that the whole attempt failed. [§2.4–2.6](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=3)

**Teaching example, not a paper example:** solving 2x + 3 = 11 correctly gives 2x = 8 and x = 4. Another trajectory mistakenly writes 2x = 6, then jumps without justification to x = 4. Checking only the final answer may label both correct; checking the derivation exposes the break. This illustrates feedback granularity, not a guarantee that a learned PRM catches every such error.

### Turning step scores into a solution score

The model predicts labels at step boundaries. One forward pass over a complete solution can provide the boundary scores; this need not mean generating a separate lengthy critique of every step. For ranking, the authors treat neutral steps as acceptable and multiply step-acceptability probabilities. Appendix F compares alternatives including minimum aggregation. [Appendix F](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=15)

**Teaching example:** candidate A has scores 0.95, 0.90, and 0.95, whose product is approximately 0.812. Candidate B has 0.99, 0.30, and 0.99, yielding approximately 0.294. B's weak middle step sharply reduces its rank. These remain model scores, not formal correctness certificates. Multiplication is sensitive to calibration and step count: splitting the same explanation into more steps can change its score. Reproduction should record segmentation, label handling, and aggregation together.

### Read the result together with the candidate budget

Large-scale experiments use generator and reward models derived from base GPT-4 on MATH, with roughly 800,000 step labels in PRM800K. The authors use 4,500 problems from the original MATH test split for training-related data and evaluate on the remaining 500 randomly held-out problems. Figure 3 reports **78.2% for PRM selection among 1,860 candidates per problem**, compared with 72.4% for ORM and 69.6% for majority voting. This is neither single-sample accuracy nor a result on the untouched full MATH test split. [§2–3 and Appendix C](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=5)

Large-scale PRM and ORM training data differ, so their entire performance gap cannot be attributed to switching label granularity alone. Separate smaller-model comparisons use synthetic supervision from a larger PRM while controlling training samples. Keep these evidence types separate: one compares strong implementations, while the other attempts to isolate supervision and depends on teacher-label quality. The paper supplies no end-to-end GPU latency multiplier that can simply be applied to a present-day deployment.

### Why informative negatives can beat more obvious errors

Collection prioritizes candidates that the current PRM scores highly but whose final answers are wrong, so annotators can expose the judge's blind spots. This differs from repeatedly adding examples it already finds easy to reject. Section 4 studies active selection with smaller models; its sample-efficiency result should not be treated as a universal reduction in complete human-labeling cost. [§2.4 and §4](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=6)

**Our experimental-design suggestion, not a paper result:** an analogous feedback loop should independently hold out evaluation tasks and record which error types each iteration adds. Fixing selected training cases may reflect adaptation to that distribution. A verifier producing labels and then judging its own progress can also make improvements appear more robust than independent tests would support.

### Connection to the main reading and limits of transfer

Today's main reading asks how to allocate inference compute; this paper explains where a trained verifier can come from. Generator quality, verifier quality, candidate count, and task success must be considered together. **Systems analysis:** a PRM adds model execution and memory demand, and stepwise search can introduce synchronization barriers. Compare total GPU time, tail latency, and cost at the same quality target rather than equating more accurate judging with faster serving.

Automatically checkable mathematics does not make every step of an open-ended agent task easy to label. Approval of a textual step also does not establish that the text faithfully describes the model's internal computation. Read §2.1 for the research object, Figures 3–4 for the experimental comparisons, and Appendices C/F for splits and scoring. Then ask: when more candidates stop improving selected-answer accuracy, should we inspect generation coverage, judge errors, or aggregation? These distinctions transfer better than memorizing 78.2%.

## Optional reading · STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning

**Year / publication：** 2022 · NeurIPS 2022 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465>)

**Reading guidance：** An optional contrast with pure inference-time computation, about 30 minutes. Read §3 and Algorithm 1 closely, then §4.1 and Table 1, and finish with the rationale-quality and bootstrapping limits in §5–6.

### Two-minute overview: turn generated reasoning into training data

STaR is a [NeurIPS 2022 paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/639a9a172c044fbb64175b5fad42e9a5-Abstract-Conference.html). Whereas Self-Consistency samples additional attempts within an inference request, STaR fine-tunes on generated reasoning and changes the model that answers future questions. It still requires many questions with correct final answers; most lack human-written rationales. Self-teaching here does not mean an absence of external supervision.

### Mechanism: generate, filter, recover failures, then train from the fixed base

A few question–rationale–answer examples prompt the current model to generate rationales and answers for training questions. Correct answers are retained. Failed questions receive rationalization: the known answer becomes a hint for another attempt, whose final answer must still pass the check. Both sets form the next training dataset. [Conference paper §3, Algorithm 1](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/639a9a172c044fbb64175b5fad42e9a5-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=3)

Each round uses the preceding model to generate data but starts fine-tuning from the same original pretrained model M, rather than continually updating the previous round's weights. Improvement passes through the new data. The answer hint is removed from the training input of recovered examples: the model learns to produce reasoning and an answer from the question. Test questions also receive no gold-answer hint.

### Teaching example: answer hints construct data, not test-time answers

**Teaching example, not a paper experiment:** The question again concerns three boxes of four pencils and two loose pencils. An initial attempt incorrectly answers 10. During data construction, the model is given the correct answer 14 and generates an explanation that first counts twelve boxed pencils and then adds two. If the final answer passes, the training input retains only the question, while the target is the rationale followed by 14.

This does not discover an unknown answer from nothing. It seeks a learnable process under existing answer supervision. A fabricated explanation could also end in 14, so answer matching filters outcomes only. Claims about trustworthy reasoning require additional step checks, executable verification where available, or human inspection; correct output and correct reasoning must remain separate criteria.

### Systems connection: track data and model versions through the loop

**Systems analysis:** Decompose a round into generation, answer checking, recovery of failures, dataset assembly, and fine-tuning. Track the generating model version, question source, recovery flag, and filtering outcome to explain changes in the next round. A fixed training initialization and an evolving data-generation model represent different version relationships; storing only a latest checkpoint loses that distinction.

For RL infrastructure, generation throughput should be considered alongside usable-example yield. Fast incorrect generations need not produce more training data, and recovery consumes additional generation work. This also connects to recursive self-improvement, or RSI, but the feedback here comes from known answers and fixed tasks. It does not establish unlimited, unsupervised improvement.

Useful logs should count examples accepted from ordinary generation, additional examples recovered, and tokens and time spent on each path. Equal usable-example counts may have very different costs. Inspect the quality of retained rationales as well: optimizing acceptance rate alone can favor answers that are easy to guess instead of examples that are valuable to learn from.

### Evidence and limits: development-set gains are not a universal reasoning guarantee

Experiments use GPT-J 6B on addition, CommonsenseQA, and GSM8K. CommonsenseQA supplies 9,741 training questions and ten manually written rationale examples. Table 1 reports development-set accuracy of 60.0% for direct-answer fine-tuning, 68.8% for STaR without rationalization, and 72.5% with it. The authors iterate until performance saturates and report the best results. [Conference paper §4.1, Table 1](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/639a9a172c044fbb64175b5fad42e9a5-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=5)

These are neither current-model rankings nor equal-compute serving comparisons. Five-way multiple-choice questions let guessed correct answers admit poor rationales into training. A base model unable to generate useful processes may also fail to bootstrap. Evaluate final-answer accuracy, rationale quality, and transfer separately; one development-set score cannot establish all three.

### Reading path: draw two model roles and two data paths

First understand supervised fine-tuning as increasing the probability of a target text given its question. Then draw the ordinary-generation and answer-hint recovery paths from Algorithm 1, labeling the training arrow as initialized from M. Continue with §4.1, Table 1, and §5–6. Ask what information the gold answers provide, what process the model generates, how easy-to-guess answers undermine filtering, and how to preserve an independent evaluation set when training questions are reused.
