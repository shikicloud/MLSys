# Training after R1: scheduling long rollouts and measuring what improves

Three 2026 preprints examine problems raised by long reasoning and sequential post-training. The main reading, CadenceRL, explains how unfinished long trajectories can pause, move between workers, and resume while accounting for waiting and state-transfer costs. VISR asks why a better score now can make a later objective harder to learn. A controlled chess study separates different claims about new abilities acquired through RL. Each note starts with a concrete example before mechanisms, evidence, and limitations; only the main paper needs a close read today.

Each digest recommends 1 main paper and 2–4 optional papers across LLM inference, RL infrastructure, post-training, RSI (recursive self-improvement), reasoning compute, agent learning and memory, data and evaluation, and model architecture. Explore foundations and current research by theme; a direct connection to current work is not required. A recommendation does not mean it has been read, and there is no need to finish everything in one day.

## Main reading · Reshaping Rollout Workloads for Asynchronous RL Post-Training on Heterogeneous Accelerators

**Year / publication：** 2026 · arXiv preprint · first submitted 2026-09-29 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36899>)

**Reading guidance：** Main reading, worth a close read in 45–60 minutes. Follow the short/long trajectory example, then Figures 8–9 for scheduling and KV movement. Read §6 and Appendix D to distinguish comparisons of scheduling policies from comparisons of complete frameworks.

### Several tasks share a worker: why can one slow task hold it back?

**Teaching example, not a paper measurement:** A worker is handling four coding tasks that start with similarly sized inputs. Later, two have passed their tests, a third is still generating, and the fourth has failed a test, read the error, and started another revision. A worker is an execution process serving a model, potentially across one or more accelerators. A rollout trajectory is the entire task, including generation and any tool interactions through completion.

The fourth trajectory accumulates context and KV state, the attention cache retained to continue generation. Leaving it where it started can eventually leave that worker serving only a few long tasks. Continually adding new tasks, however, can delay its completion further. CadenceRL permits saving its progress, removing it from the current worker, and later resuming it elsewhere. It reorganizes unfinished work instead of discarding slow samples.

The paper comes from Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications and StepFun, with its first public version dated September 29, 2026; it is treated here as a preprint. The systems question extends beyond overlapping generation and training: a rollout pool must also handle a continuously changing mixture of short and long work. These attribution and date details were checked against the [PDF title page](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=1) and [version record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36899).

### Why total token throughput and one trajectory’s completion time can disagree

A decode step reads model weights and accesses each trajectory’s historical KV. Batching several trajectories can amortize weight reads, increasing the worker’s total tokens per second, or throughput. Those trajectories also share memory bandwidth. A long task in a crowded batch need not make rapid progress individually. [Paper §2–3, Figures 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=3)

The proposed response is for workers to develop different workload compositions: some keep many shorter contexts active, while others concentrate resources on long trajectories. This is not a permanent partition into two teams. Tasks finish, contexts grow, and worker roles change. Long reasoning and agent training after o1/R1 make this problem particularly relevant; this paper advances scheduling, rather than proposing a new reasoning-learning algorithm.

The paper describes removing long work and replacing it with shorter work as a direction with guaranteed gain. That argument depends on the assumed decode-throughput landscape and the combined replacement. It does not make every migration beneficial or automatically account for queues, KV transport, recomputation, and final training quality. Whether the full arrangement is worthwhile remains an empirical question.

### Let a trajectory leave before choosing where it resumes

When the fourth task in the example pauses, the best destination may not yet be known. CadenceRL first moves it into a pending set, retaining generated content and the information needed for recovery. It assigns a destination when suitable space is available. A trajectory can therefore alternate between active generation, pending placement, and resumed generation. Its execution has several segments, but it remains one logical trajectory. [Paper §4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=6)

Pending sets are indexed by model-weight version. **CadenceRL chooses to keep one policy version throughout each trajectory**, so it resumes only on a worker serving that version. A worker can change weights after its current residents finish or depart. This is a design choice, not a universal requirement imposed on every PPO/GRPO system; the paper’s AReaL baseline changes versions when resuming segments.

Placement first requires enough cache capacity. The paper uses KV headroom to describe available capacity after existing state is accounted for, with thresholds controlling admission. Think of it as the remaining room for cached computation. CadenceRL decides which trajectories reside on each worker; the inference engine still handles local token batching and memory management.

### While short work is available, free space and refill it carefully

The first mechanism is called pacing. If a worker’s KV headroom falls below a floor, the scheduler removes its longest current context; it repeats if more space is needed. Pending work is then considered from shorter to longer current contexts. This uses accumulated history, not a prediction of which question is easier or how much generation remains. [Paper §4.2, Figure 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=7)

**Teaching example:** Ignoring prefix reuse, suppose two compatible workers can accept a short task. Placement would leave one nearly full and the other with a large free region. Tight-fit placement selects the former, preserving the larger region for long work. Appendix Algorithm 1 additionally prefers the worker that last served the same prompt when it is still feasible, exploiting shared prefixes. Tight fit applies when that suitable sticky destination is unavailable. [Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=15)

Fitting now is insufficient because continued generation grows KV. The placement threshold therefore adds a per-trajectory growth reserve, δ, while eviction uses the lower headroom floor. The gap reduces immediate eviction after admission. Removing long-context residents and replacing them with shorter work together constitute the throughput-maintenance mechanism.

### When admission stops, consolidate the remaining tail

The system cannot admit unlimited new trajectories while training keeps updating the policy: old-version data can become increasingly stale. Once the admission budget is exhausted and there is insufficient fresh short work for pacing, the scheduler switches to concentration. It evacuates workers one at a time and places residual long tasks on devices better suited to finish them. [Paper §4.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=8)

This stage uses a configured hardware-affinity ordering: H800 above BI-V150 above A910X in the experiments. The ranking is not learned automatically online. A weaker long-context worker is preferentially emptied; long tasks are placed first on higher-affinity targets with capacity. A target may itself evict shorter residents to make room. Consolidation proceeds incrementally rather than scattering the entire pool’s work at once.

To prevent an expanding pending backlog, the appendix algorithm waits for previously displaced work to be placed before evicting the next trajectory from the source. Once empty, the source can advance its weight version while other workers finish older work. It may first join an intermediate version that still needs help with its tail, rather than immediately jumping to the globally newest version. Actual absorption of displaced work provides feedback instead of an assumption that destinations always have room.

### The admission budget limits new work, not each trajectory’s age

While the trainer updates its weights, a still-running trajectory can continue under an older version. This difference is policy staleness. The implementation uses admission credits: each completed training iteration replenishes an iteration-batch of sample credits, and η controls how far generation can run ahead of training consumption. In steady state, in-flight samples are bounded at roughly `(η+1)` iteration-batches. [Paper §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=9)

This controls the rate of new work, rather than guaranteeing a maximum age for every trajectory. Consequently, η=1 should not be rewritten as a hard promise that every sample is at most one policy version old. A larger budget can overlap more work but may also keep displaced long trajectories waiting longer; it is not simply a throughput knob.

The budget also explains the two scheduling modes. Available short work sustains larger generation batches. When admission tightens and that supply runs out, completing old work takes priority so workers can advance. Parameter choices should therefore be evaluated against waiting time and training outcomes, not token counts alone.

### How to reduce prefix recomputation when a trajectory moves

If a destination receives only the generated text, it must recompute the prefix before decoding can continue. To reduce that cost, CadenceRL first snapshots departing trajectories’ KV from device memory into host memory. Before a destination is selected, chosen prefix ranges are staged to relay nodes through RDMA, used here to transfer memory data between nodes. [Paper §4.4, Figure 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=9)

After placement, the destination builds a loading plan and fetches prefix ranges in parallel from local or remote host memory. The destination is selected late while state preparation starts earlier. Scheduling need not lock in a receiver prematurely merely to begin preparation; the two activities can progress separately. Transport still has a cost.

**Prepared KV transfer applies to compatible workers within the same cluster. Cross-cluster or cross-device-type migration falls back to sending tokens and re-prefilling at the destination.** It is therefore not universal zero-recomputation migration across heterogeneous devices. A natural pause while an agent waits for a tool can also let a trajectory leave device memory, but the tool environment’s own execution state is separate from model KV.

### What models, workloads, and hardware were actually evaluated?

The prototype uses vLLM for inference and Steptron for training. PPO runs start from Qwen3-8B, 14B, and 32B checkpoints supervised-fine-tuned on the task mixture, with maximum contexts of 64K, 32K, and 16K respectively. Tasks cover mathematics, competitive programming, STEM, and logical puzzles, combining single-turn generation with multi-turn interactions. A global batch has 512 prompts and 16 responses each, or 8,192 samples; each iteration performs one mini-batch update, and trajectories allow up to 12 tool calls. [Paper §5–6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=9)

Rollout uses H800, A910X, and BI-V150 devices, while training stays on H800. Environment and reward services run on separate CPU clusters. Connectivity is 8×200 Gbps RDMA within clusters and a 20 Gbps dedicated link between clusters. Device capacities, bandwidths, and costs cannot be reduced to one undifferentiated GPU count. Homogeneous H800 experiments use 16–64 total devices depending on model and configuration, including trainer devices. [Table 1 and Appendix C, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=18)

**The trainer is deliberately given spare capacity so that rollout is the throughput bottleneck.** These measurements primarily isolate generation-scheduling efficiency, rather than establish whole-training speedups for arbitrary resource splits. Trajectory latency runs from its first inference step through completion and includes pending-placement time. P95 is the completion-time threshold within which 95% of trajectories finish. It includes waiting; time actively executing on a device alone is insufficient.

### Read the baselines first, then separate homogeneous and heterogeneous results

Baselines are synchronous execution, one-step off-policy overlap, DORA*, Laminar, and AReaL. **All are coordination policies implemented in the same underlying framework, not a ranking from running each complete public project.** DORA* implements only workload-aware worker reallocation at weight-synchronization points; the asterisk matters. It is excluded from heterogeneous comparisons. This design helps isolate scheduling while limiting claims about outperforming entire frameworks. [Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=18)

Figures 10–11 show a throughput–latency trade-off on homogeneous H800 pools: shorter trajectory tails can still cost aggregate throughput. The heterogeneous comparison in Figure 12 uses Qwen3-8B with rollout TP=2 on A910X/BI-V150 and TP=1 on H800. AReaL and CadenceRL both use η=1, with δ=512 for CadenceRL. Across those configurations, the plotted CadenceRL results match or exceed baseline throughput while reducing trajectory latency. These conditions belong with the conclusion. [Paper Figures 10–12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=10)

One exactly tabulated waiting metric is useful: for pools of 32 rollout devices, Table 3 gives the P95 fraction of a long-context trajectory’s lifetime spent non-resident, waiting for placement, as 39.91% in 32A and 80.94% in 32B. A and B denote A910X and BI-V150. This illustrates how substantial waiting can be. It is not a speedup between algorithms, and the difference in fractions cannot be treated as a difference in trajectory durations. [Appendix E, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=19)

### Evidence boundaries: parameters, transfer costs, and training quality

Figure 15 varies η on Qwen3-32B: throughput improves from 1 to 3, then declines as η increases further, while latency increases. Figure 16 varies δ from 512 to 4,096 tokens on 14B: throughput is relatively stable, but tail latency rises. More in-flight work or greater reserved capacity is therefore not universally preferable; these trends also belong to the tested configurations. [Paper §6.3, Figures 15–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=11)

Figure 18 examines KV preparation on 8B. With two relay nodes, throughput changes have different signs in pacing and concentration: a modest benefit in the former and a modest loss in the latter. Avoiding prefix recomputation does not remove preparation and transfer expenses. This mechanism should not be treated as a free optimization. [Paper §6.4, Figure 18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=12)

Training-quality evidence needs particular care. Figure 17’s caption specifies Qwen3-8B, η=1 for asynchronous strategies, and 30 training iterations, with similar mean-reward curves; its horizontal axis is nevertheless labeled in hours. No convergence-time estimate is inferred here from that inconsistency. Similar short-run training rewards do not establish unchanged final capability for larger models, larger η, or longer runs, and do not replace downstream evaluation. [Paper Figure 17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.36899v1#page=12)

### Bring it back to your system: track where a task waits, not just device activity

**My systems inference:** Before trying a similar scheduler, record one slow trajectory’s timeline: generation, tool waiting, departure from a worker, KV transfer or re-prefill, resumption, and completion, together with the weight version used at each point. This tests whether spending less time resident actually produces earlier completion, rather than merely changing utilization.

Then compare total pool token throughput, per-trajectory completion time, migration bytes, and training quality together. Migration requires checking not only policy versions but compatibility between the saved state and destination implementation; this motivates the paper’s distinction between direct KV loading and re-prefill. If training rather than rollout is your bottleneck, results obtained with deliberate trainer spare capacity do not directly establish an iteration-level gain.

Read Figure 2 for the training loop, Figure 8 for one scheduling cycle, Figure 9 for the cache path, and Appendix B for the algorithms; then verify the evidence in §6 and Appendix D. The useful questions are which workers should process more short tasks, which should help long tasks finish, and how to maintain that division as execution changes while accounting for waiting and transport. These are more transferable than a maximum speedup number.

## Optional reading · Verifier-Induced Support Reshaping in On-Policy Optimization

**Year / publication：** 2026 · arXiv 2026 · v2 2026-09-29 · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00220>)

**Reading guidance：** Worth a close read; allow 25–35 minutes. Start with the example of two questions answered 32 times each, then read Figure 2, Table 1, Figure 4, and Figure 8 before checking the experimental scope.

### Why learning more now can make the next stage harder

Ask a model to generate several responses to a question, check them with a program, and train it to generate higher-scoring responses more often. This is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR; each complete generation is a rollout. Because the current model generates the training data, what the next stage can learn also depends on what the current model can still produce.

VISR asks whether training math and then format compliance, or doing these in the opposite order, can make successful responses for the other objective too difficult to sample. It calls the successful responses reachable within a specified number of attempts effective rewardable support. This extends the post-R1 discussion to sequential training rather than just one task score. This note uses v2 of the preprint, first submitted on July 31, 2026 and revised on September 29. [Version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00220); [paper §1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2) The authors are affiliated with Peking University and BUPT. [Team attribution](https://github.com/sylvain-wei/VISR/blob/a63f00f3aded450871ed8655e402bbb13373a099/README.md).

### Answer two questions 32 times each: two scores can move in opposite directions

**This is an invented example explaining the metrics, not paper data.** Answer questions A and B 32 times each. Before training, A succeeds 16 times and B succeeds four times. After training, A succeeds all 32 times and B never succeeds. Across the 64 responses, the success fraction rises from 20/64, or 31.25%, to 50%. This estimates pass@1, the success probability of a single attempt.

Now ask a different question: for each question, did at least one of the 32 attempts succeed? Both questions met that condition before training; only A does afterward. The fraction falls from 100% to 50%. The paper calls this best@32. A checker establishes whether a successful response exists; this metric does not mean a deployed system already knows how to select that response from the 32 candidates.

The model can become more reliable on questions where success is easier while making occasional success harder on another set. If the next training stage relies on the model to generate its own successful examples, question B now supplies less useful material. That is why a higher average score does not establish that subsequent training will be easier. [Paper §3 and Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

### How the experiments test this pattern

The authors start separately from Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen2.5-Math-7B. For each starting model, they train math and instruction-following branches, then continue each with the opposite objective. Math training uses the 7,500-question MATH training split and checks final answers. Instruction-following training uses IFTrain and requires every explicit response constraint to pass, such as format, length, or keyword requirements. IF abbreviates instruction following. [Paper §3 and Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

On the IFEval instruction-following benchmark, Qwen3 after math training gains 6.5 percentage points in single-attempt success but loses 9.8 points in best@32. Qwen2.5-Math gains 7.9 points and loses 11.4 points, respectively. These are changes from each starting checkpoint. Both models show the same directional pattern on a second instruction benchmark, IFBench, so the finding is not limited to one selected curve. [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

### The next stage needs successful and unsuccessful answers it can compare

The group-relative update used here compares responses to the same question. If some receive reward 1 and others reward 0, their differences identify which responses should become more likely. If every response fails, or every response succeeds, that group has no reward variation. This concerns the within-group signal supplied by rewards; it does not assert that every term in the training objective has zero gradient.

After instruction-following training, continued math training still makes progress: over 20 steps, the share of diagnostic prompts with only failed attempts falls from 65.6% to 50.8%. But the share with both successes and failures also falls from 33.6% to 1.6%, while the all-success share rises to 47.7%. Most prompts therefore end up at one of two extremes: reliably successful or still yielding no successful response. [Paper §4.2 and Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

These are diagnostic samples at the start and step 20, collected without filtering out prompts beforehand; actual training separately uses dynamic sampling. They do not show that almost every training batch lacks a learning signal, and they cannot establish a percentage of wasted GPU time. The evidence is that this short continuation does not rebuild a substantial set of prompts offering both success and failure, not that further training must always fail. [Appendix B.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

### Why the investigation reaches the first piece of generated text

A model generates tokens, the units into which text is split, one at a time. The authors give the models before and after RLVR the same already-written prefix and compare their probabilities for what comes next, avoiding comparisons between different contexts. The largest average change occurs at the first generated token in every tested combination. Math training favors openings that begin a stepwise solution; instruction training favors openings that state an answer immediately. [Paper §5.1–5.2 and Figures 6–7](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

An opening changing alongside a score is not enough to establish causation. The authors therefore leave model parameters unchanged and force an instruction-trained model to start with a token favored by its original model, or with a stepwise-reasoning prefix, before allowing free generation. Math best@32 improves for both models. This shows that choosing the opening can affect subsequent responses in these settings. [Paper §5.3 and Figure 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

The reverse intervention is not consistent across models: forcing an original model toward a direct answer hurts Qwen3 but has neutral or positive effects on Qwen2.5-Math. The result is therefore not a universal instruction to add a step-by-step phrase. These labels describe visible text, not whether reasoning occurs internally, and they do not establish irreversible deletion of mathematical ability.

### Why several natural remedies still leave the problem open

Could training simply prevent the model from changing too much? The authors use a KL constraint, which penalizes differences between the new model's output probabilities and a reference model's probabilities. A stronger constraint retains more math performance but reduces instruction-following gains. Another approach first teaches stepwise openings using 50 correct math responses. This delays the shift, but all tested conditions favor direct-answer openings by the endpoint of the 100-step instruction-training experiment. [Paper §4.3 and §6.1, Figures 5 and 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

They also test on-policy distillation: the student generates its own responses, the teacher supplies probabilities for possible next tokens at each step, and the student learns from them. This provides more feedback than a final correct-or-incorrect label, but results depend on which stage of the teacher's training supplies the checkpoint. The teacher best for instruction transfer is not the teacher best for math retention. Denser feedback does not automatically remove the conflict between the objectives. [Paper §6.2 and Figures 10–11](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

### What is useful now, and what the evidence still cannot establish

This is a diagnostic study, not a generally effective repair. It tests two models, with each training and stochastic-evaluation configuration run once using random seed 42. Repeated generation does not replace independent training runs. Nor does observing no correct response within a finite number of attempts establish that its probability is zero. [Paper §3 and Appendices A–B](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

**The following is a practical suggestion inferred from the results:** during sequential post-training, supplement the current average reward with a fixed-budget check of how many prompts for the next task still yield occasional success. Using separate checkpoints, the paper also evaluates MathIF and ReasonIF, which require the same response to be correct and obey instructions. Improvements in the individual properties only partly translate into simultaneous success; this test does not extend the earlier continuation curves. [Paper §7 and Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00220v2)

For a close read, start with §3 for the measurements, then Figure 2 and Table 1 for the opposing trends, Figure 4 for subsequent learning, and Figure 8 for the interventions. Finish with §6 and Appendices A–B. The official repository provides training, evaluation, and analysis code, but explicitly does not bundle the paper checkpoints or raw rollouts. Available code is not the same as a complete reproduction that can be run immediately after download. [Official repository, commit a63f00f checked on 2026-10-17](https://github.com/sylvain-wei/VISR/blob/a63f00f3aded450871ed8655e402bbb13373a099/README.md)

## Optional reading · Understanding Reasoning from Pretraining to Post-Training

**Year / publication：** 2026 · Preprint · July 2026 / v2 August 2026 · NYU, Modal Labs, UCLA, UIUC, Columbia · [Original source](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16097v2>)

**Reading guidance：** Optional and worth reading: §2, Fig. 2, §4.1 / Fig. 5, and Appendix A, followed by the mathematical-language experiment in §5. Allow 30–40 minutes. Focus on separating different claims about new abilities before working through the scaling equations.

### Does choosing the right move more often mean acquiring a new ability?

Imagine a chess position where a model strongly prefers move A and occasionally chooses B, although B is correct. After training, it chooses B more often. **This is an explanatory example, not a measured result from the paper.** We still do not know whether an occasional success has become reliable or whether the model has learned a new way to judge the position. Final accuracy alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The authors study chess because individual decisions can be inspected. They compare the probabilities of legal moves before and after training and examine the continuations the model considered. A probability describes a model's preference in this position; it does not directly establish whether related positions appeared in its training data.

The paper is by Jingyan Shen, Ang Li, Pavel Izmailov, and colleagues, with affiliations including NYU, Modal Labs, UCLA, UIUC, and Columbia. The first version appeared on July 17, 2026. This note uses v2, dated August 9, and labels the work as a preprint. [Version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16097v2); [official code](https://github.com/pavelslab-nyu/pre2post-chess).

### Learn games, learn to consider continuations, then practice against outcomes

First, the model reads human games and predicts subsequent moves: this is pretraining. Moves are encoded as tokens, the basic symbols processed by the model, using a vocabulary of only 81 symbols. These models are trained from scratch with the Qwen3 network architecture; they are not existing Qwen3 language models that have already absorbed a large text corpus.

Second, demonstrations show several possible continuations followed by a selected move. Learning to imitate these demonstrations is supervised fine-tuning, or SFT. In the main description, a pretrained model generates possible lines, shared beginnings are merged into a tree, and the paths are written into a training sequence. The resulting reasoning trace consists of chess moves, not natural-language explanations. Calling it a chain of thought does not establish human-like explanatory ability.

Third, the model attempts puzzles. An incorrect move ends the attempt; a correct move causes the environment to provide the prescribed opponent response. Completing the entire solution earns 1, and any error earns 0. GRPO reinforcement learning compares outcomes within a group of attempts on the same problem and adjusts the model weights—the learned numbers governing generation—to make successful behavior more likely. These updates change the model used on future problems. [§2, Fig. 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16097v2).

### Which models and datasets are actually compared?

The pretraining pool contains 54 billion chess tokens, with models ranging from 5 million to 1 billion parameters. A post-training pool contains 156,000 puzzles; Appendix C.1 specifies subsets of 42,000 for SFT and 69,300 for RL. Evaluation uses 1,480 puzzles across five difficulty bins. The authors report separation at the board-position level, reducing direct position leakage without establishing that every relevant tactic or solution pattern is unfamiliar. [§2.4, Appendix C.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16097v2).

The joint pretraining–RL study contains 36 runs using 20M, 50M, 200M, and 680M models, each receiving 1,000–5,000 RL steps. The pre-RL baseline is the corresponding model after SFT. **Main aggregate scores include only the first four difficulty bins**: the models rarely solve the hardest fifth bin, which is retained for analysis by difficulty. The main curves therefore do not average over the full hard-puzzle set. [§3.2, Fig. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16097v2#page=5).

### What happens to correct moves that were previously unlikely?

Figure 5 separates several changes. On easier puzzles, an already highly ranked correct move often becomes more reliable. On harder puzzles, an unlikely correct move may rise into the leading choices, but an incorrect move may also become more strongly preferred. Selecting only the favorable cases would not justify a claim that RL always creates new abilities.

The definition is concrete. Appendix H.3 analyzes a 50M model pretrained on 4.6 billion tokens. A correct move counts as tail discovery when its SFT probability is below 5%, it initially lies outside the top three, and RL promotes it into the top three. This means elevating a low-probability answer, not creating an answer from zero probability. It also does not establish that the training data lacked related knowledge.

The authors generate 128 reasoning traces per position to estimate the overall move probabilities, so the estimate also has sampling error. A separate analysis of 20M and 50M models finds broader exploration of possible continuations, while deeper planning remains difficult. Considering more branches does not automatically mean looking further ahead. [§4, Appendix H, Figs. 5 and 22–25](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16097v2).

### A more reliable first attempt versus solving more problems with repeated attempts

Pass@1 measures the chance that one complete attempt succeeds. Pass@16 measures the chance that at least one of 16 attempts succeeds; it is not the accuracy of a majority-voted answer delivered to a user. In Fig. 2, RL consistently improves the former, while the latter is often flat or slightly worse for the larger chess models. Greater reliability on one attempt therefore does not guarantee that a fixed budget of repeated attempts solves more problems.

The authors also compare spending a total compute budget on pretraining versus RL. Within the studied range, stronger pretraining often supports a better RL starting point and better subsequent performance. The preferred allocation nevertheless depends on total budget and the evaluation metric. The predictive equations in §3.3 describe trends over the measured range; they do not establish unlimited improvement from additional RL or directly prescribe a budget for frontier models. [§3, Figs. 2–4, Appendix G.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16097v2).

### What the math experiment adds—and what it does not test

The authors also train an OLMo-2 mathematical-language model. The main text calls it 1B; Table 15 reports 1.48 billion total and 1.07 billion non-embedding parameters. One main training run consumes 200 billion tokens. Fourteen intermediate model states between 10 billion and 200 billion tokens each receive an additional 5 billion tokens while the learning rate is reduced, followed by separate SFT and RL. These are not fourteen independent pretraining runs.

Section 5 describes a mixture of 70% Nemotron-CC-Math-v1 and 30% Dolma3, whereas Table 16 says Dolma3/Dolmino mix: the descriptions are inconsistent. ### Why read it now, and how does it connect to systems work?

The paper directly studies how RL training changes reasoning behavior, but its chess environment has a small vocabulary, a designated unique solution, and a binary reward. Open-ended answers, tool use, and frontier language models introduce substantially different conditions. The useful reading habit is to distinguish single-attempt success, repeated-attempt success, promotion of unlikely answers, and actual generalization to untrained problems. [Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16097v2).

**The following systems connection is our interpretation:** before comparing two RL recipes, align their starting models, data, and evaluation procedures, then measure the time spent generating attempts and updating weights. The paper allocates compute mainly in FLOPs, the number of floating-point operations. Equal operation counts need not imply equal GPU time; environment waits and parallel execution still require measurement.

Start with Fig. 1 for the training process, compare the two success metrics in Fig. 2, and inspect the three changes in Fig. 5. Then read Fig. 6 and Appendix A to see which findings extend to math and which remain confined to chess. Returning to the equations in §3 afterward makes their prediction target clearer without requiring the scaling terminology first.
